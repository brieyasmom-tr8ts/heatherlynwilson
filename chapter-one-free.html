Built to Shine
For the Woman Leading with Faith in the Business World
Heather Wilson
This is Chapter One. The full book releases October 15, 2026.

Chapter One

The Lie of Permission

Lie: I need permission before I obey.

There’s a particular kind of leadership a lot of women know very well.

You’re running the thing without anyone ever saying you’re running it. You’re making decisions, training volunteers, answering questions, smoothing conflicts, and you’re the one people text when something needs to happen. And if you stopped showing up, the whole thing would probably fall apart inside a week.

But your name isn’t on anything. There’s no title on your email signature and no salary attached to your work. You aren’t officially in charge.

So you keep going. You keep carrying it. And every now and then, you ask the question to yourself or to your husband or to God:

Why am I doing all of this without permission to call it what it is? Why can I be trusted with the responsibility but not with the role?

That question is where this chapter starts, because it’s where the lie starts. Somewhere along the way, a lot of us came to believe that we needed permission before we obeyed.

✦✦✦

I didn’t grow up lacking confidence.

From a young age, leadership felt natural to me, though I wouldn’t have called it that at the time. What I knew was that I liked being trusted with responsibility and figuring out how to steward it well. I didn’t crave attention as much as I craved being useful. There was something deeply satisfying about being needed.

One of my earliest memories of this was sometime in middle school. I attended the children’s ministry at my church. One week I was asked if I would be interested in teaching an upcoming lesson. I still remember it clearly. The children’s pastor handed me a picture of a lightning bug with a short lesson and a Bible verse on the back about shining your light. I was told I could take it home, prepare what I wanted to say, and come back ready to teach the other kids.

I loved it! I wasn’t overwhelmed and I wasn’t nervous—I was excited! I spent the week preparing. I felt honored to have been asked, and not because I was given some kind of title or because anyone applauded me. I felt honored because I was given permission.

I don’t remember whether everyone eventually got a turn. What I do remember is how it felt to be entrusted with something meaningful, to be recognized as someone who could handle the responsibility. A leader looked at me and believed I was capable of teaching, of leading, of contributing. That moment mattered enough that I can still remember it over thirty-five years later.

That experience affirmed something in me that I would carry for years. Leadership wasn’t about age or authority. It was about stewardship. If you were faithful with what you were given, you could influence the space around you and more opportunities would open up.

As I moved into high school, that pattern continued. Our youth group had a student leadership team, and it wasn’t just in name. There was real responsibility involved. We helped plan events, discussed direction, and talked about culture and how the group functioned. The adult leaders took us seriously and held us to a high standard. When we messed up, they corrected us and gave us trust and direction as we moved forward.

I learned countless lessons on what it looked like to serve. Those years also taught me that vision shapes people and consistency builds trust. Leadership wasn’t something I aspired to someday, it was something I was already practicing.

Those early experiences shaped me more than I realized at the time. I watched the leaders around me influence culture without demanding attention, and I saw how a whole environment could shift when one person quietly decided to take responsibility for it.

They also taught me something more subtle, something I wouldn’t name until years later. There was a clear, predictable rhythm: step up, be reliable, and add value. When you did, you were entrusted with more, and your voice carried weight. It felt empowering. It was a pattern that for a long time, seemed both right and good.

I wouldn’t have said any of that if you had asked me at the time, but it was forming the framework. In my mind, leadership happened when someone invited you, influence grew through validation, and belonging followed contribution. And I saw it play out around me, too. Once you stopped being useful, or stopped contributing the way the people in charge wanted, you could lose all of it. You could lose the invitation.

And obedience to God? Obedience followed the invitation. It followed someone seeing something in me first.

I didn’t realize any of that at the time. It felt healthy. But underneath it I was being trained to move when trusted and to wait or try harder when overlooked. I was learning to lead when called and to hesitate when silence followed. I was learning that permission preceded action.

That belief would follow me into rooms much bigger than a children’s ministry classroom.

When affirmation was present, I moved confidently. When it was absent, I paused. I assumed the pause meant something. I assumed silence was direction. If no one had asked or affirmed me, maybe it wasn’t mine to step into yet. I began treating closed doors and silence as direction, assuming that if no one invited me in, it wasn’t my role to knock.

The lie didn’t begin as insecurity. It began as structure.

I had experienced the blessing of being entrusted and the joy of responsibility, and I had seen what happened when faithfulness was recognized. The problem was that I began to equate affirmation with authority. When someone believed in me or handed me responsibility, I moved confidently. Their validation felt like an invitation into action.

But what happened when no one recognized what I had to offer? What if the calling God placed on me was internal, conviction came before consensus, and obedience required me to step forward without anyone else’s approval?

That’s where the tension would eventually surface. And it always did. I’ve seen this pattern in my own life and in the lives of other women, too many times to count.

Because the Kingdom of God doesn’t always work the way our systems do. God doesn’t always wait for someone else to validate what He has already spoken. Calling doesn’t always come with applause. Sometimes calling comes with discomfort or resistance. Other times it comes as a nudge and asks you to move before anyone else understands why.

And if you have trained yourself to wait for permission from someone other than God, that moment can feel paralyzing.

I can see now that my early leadership experiences were gifts. They shaped me and gave me confidence in ways I didn’t fully understand at the time. They taught me responsibility and helped prepare me for what came later. I wouldn’t trade those years for anything.

But they also planted something in me that I didn’t see for a long time. Slowly, I began to confuse affirmation with authority, and that’s where the lie began: the lie that I needed permission before I fully obeyed.

✦✦✦

That belief followed me right into adulthood.

After Dan and I got married, we made a conscious decision to find a church that would be our own, not one of the churches our families attended or any we had grown up in. We wanted something chosen together, not inherited. A place where our faith would deepen because we were investing in it together, not just continuing what was familiar.

We visited several churches in our area. Some left us encouraged and some left us unsure, and we would process in the car afterward what felt aligned and what didn’t. We were looking for a place where we could grow, serve, and build real community. Not just attend, but belong. Eventually we found one that felt like a good fit, and we hopped right in. When the doors were open, we showed up. We built relationships, made friends quickly and deeply, joined a young married couples Sunday school class, and felt like we were part of the family there.

Before long, Dan was asked to help with music. He’s a gifted musician and vocalist, and someone noticed. At the time, it was a simple ask: Would he be willing to lead a short song time before Sunday school? Just a few songs of praise and worship before people were dismissed to their classes. He said yes.

He showed up faithfully. Dan on the keyboard, leading people in worship. That gift became clearer to other people too. He was mentored by a close friend, David, who was serving as the worship pastor at the time. His confidence grew and his calling sharpened. What started small slowly turned into something more defined, and eventually his leadership was recognized formally. So, he stepped out of his IT job, and into a worship pastor role.

At the same time, I found my place in the children’s ministry.

It was a natural fit for me. I came from a large family, the oldest girl in a family of twelve kids, and I had already served in children’s ministry at previous churches. When I saw a small children’s program at our new church, with fewer than ten kids and loosely structured with no real leader or direction, I did what I loved to do. I stepped in without hesitation and got to work.

I started teaching and getting to know the kids, then began choosing curriculum, building systems, recruiting volunteers, and casting vision for the ministry. I cared deeply about the families and the spiritual growth of the kids we were serving. As the ministry grew, I began to see where people fit best. There were volunteers who connected naturally with kids, others who could organize almost anything, and a few who were ready to step up and lead. I learned to trust people with real responsibility and give them room to lead.

Before long, it wasn’t just me carrying the ministry. We had teams of people helping lead VBS, Easter programs, kids choir, a mime group, and so much more. What had once felt fragile became stable because people were invested, they knew they mattered, and they were willing to give themselves to the families of that church.

In my mid-twenties, I wasn’t officially on staff, but I was functioning as the leader of one of the church’s largest ministries.

I was in the meetings, shaping plans, fielding questions, and carrying responsibility when things needed attention. If a volunteer didn’t show up, it landed on my plate. When the ministry needed direction, people looked to me for input. There was no formal title attached to my role, but the weight was real, and I carried it willingly.

At the time, I was thriving, and God was using me to build something new.

And then came the t-shirt.

The staff had t-shirts made for a campaign we were running. Everyone who was on staff received one. As they were handed out around the table, I realized they had not ordered me one.

I understood the logistics. I wasn’t on payroll. My name wasn’t technically on the staff list. It was simply how the order had been placed. There was no malice in it. No intentional exclusion. If I had asked for one, I’m sure someone would have ordered one for me without hesitation.

And yet, the moment landed heavily on me that day.

I knew it wasn’t really about the shirt. It was the realization that I was close enough to be included when work needed to be done and to be invited into planning meetings. I was asked to carry responsibility and to represent the ministry publicly. But still held just outside the circle of formal belonging.

I stood there watching everyone else put theirs on, smiling and laughing, a team sold out to the mission, and the sting of being left out hit… hard. I was hurt and angry. The frustration rose faster than I expected. For a brief moment I just wanted to quit. I wanted to step back and say, If I’m not really part of this, then maybe I should stop carrying it like I am.

The thoughts came quickly, charged with emotion: Why am I giving so much if I’m still on the outside? Why am I leading without being recognized as a leader? If I’m trusted with responsibility, why am I not trusted with belonging? Those questions began to shape the answer I was already reaching for. I thought recognition would fix it. That if someone would finally name what I was already doing, maybe I would feel like I belonged.

None of it was about just cotton and ink. It was about what the shirt represented, a visible declaration of who was in. Who we recognize as part of the team. I had assumed that I belonged. That I had permission to be part of the leadership staff at the church. But at that moment, it felt like I didn’t.

I still remember it today, all these years later, because it exposed something deeper in me. I had tied my willingness to carry responsibility to the hope that one day it would be formally acknowledged. I told myself I was serving purely. And in many ways, I was. I loved watching kids come to know Jesus. I was excited about seeing my leaders grow and flourish. But I didn’t realize that the framework underneath my leadership was a quiet expectation that faithfulness and hard work would eventually secure my place.

Standing there in that meeting, I realized how much I longed for something official, something that said I wasn’t just useful, I belonged.

And when that visible marker didn’t come, it shook me, and I picked up that hurt and carried it.

It was such a small detail in the scope of ministry life. No one else likely remembers it. But for me, it marked a shift. It revealed how closely I had tied belonging to people’s affirmation. How much I believed that if I was truly meant to lead, someone would eventually make it official.

✦✦✦

Over and over again, this same pattern repeated itself at different churches.

Dan would step into a paid ministry role, and I would step into a volunteer leadership role in children’s ministry. I would pour myself into it completely. And eventually I would ask what felt like a reasonable question: Could this be my official job? Could I have a title? Could I be paid? I’m already doing the work.

The answer was always no. Always.

Sometimes it was framed gently. Other times it was blunt. We aren’t hiring for that role. We have more important positions to fill first. They would fill me with words of affirmation because they needed me, but the words started meaning less and less over time. Often, after I stepped out of the role, they would go on to hire someone for that same position.

The most confusing part wasn’t the rejection. It was the contradiction. I was put in a position to lead but not given the title or compensation that would usually follow.

I believed I was doing exactly what God had called me to do, and yet the door I expected to open, the one I wanted, the one I kept holding out hope for, never did.

There was one time when I decided I was going to ask for a title, without even the expectation of being paid.

At that point I had been helping build the fourth and fifth grade ministry at a church averaging over 1,500 that was beginning to experience real growth. The children’s ministry had reached a point where age groups needed to be separated to serve kids more intentionally, and I was asked to take responsibility for the fourth and fifth graders. I was given a room to decorate with a budget to make it cool. I was given freedom to pick the curriculum and build a leadership team.

I stepped into that role with energy and clarity. Kids were inviting friends. Attendance was growing. We started something we called Club 45. Nights that were simply about fun, connection, and building community. We also created intentional discipleship time where we opened Scripture together and talked about what faith looked like in our everyday life.

A lot was happening. And I was thriving.

My kids were getting older, and I was starting to think about what I would love to be doing as full-time work. As I looked ahead, it felt natural to ask whether the children’s ministry role I was already faithfully carrying could become my actual job.

I also deeply respected the children’s pastor I was working with. She was an incredible woman of God. She was a supportive leader and friend. I trusted her, so I went to her first.

I asked if I could step into the role of assistant children’s pastor. I told her I wasn’t asking for compensation. I just wanted the title. I understood how systems worked, and I knew that if I ever pursued ministry work formally in the future, experience and titles would matter. I wanted my resume to reflect what I was already doing.

I told her I would continue doing the work I was doing and was willing to take on anything else a job description would require. She listened intently and told me she would need to bring the request to the pastor.

When the answer came back, it was no.

There wasn’t much explanation. No clear reason given. I was told that there was another area in the church that needed help with growth, and that I could volunteer there if I wanted to continue developing.

I remember feeling genuinely confused.

Nothing about my work or the ministry was being questioned. In fact, it was growing and thriving. At the time, I couldn’t fully name what felt so unsettling about that conversation. Now I understand it clearly.

I wasn’t being told I lacked ability, faithfulness or fruit:

I was being told I lacked human permission.

I see now this wasn’t an isolated moment. It was a pattern that repeated itself for years and reinforced the belief that someone else had to authorize what God had already placed in me.

✦✦✦

But with distance and discernment, I came to understand that whole season differently.

I no longer wonder what those closed doors meant. I know now that God was shutting the door in children’s ministry, and not as a rejection but as preparation.

At the time, I think I could feel the disconnect. The encouraging words would keep me feeling needed, but week after week, I was giving hours of unpaid work to lead a ministry that, in most churches our size, would have been a full-time position. But now I can see the kindness in those closed doors. If I had been serving as a full time children’s leader when the opportunity for GiveSendGo came along, I would have had to say no. I would have already been doing the work I loved most. My capacity would have been spoken for. Instead, God was keeping me available.

Those years weren’t wasted. They were a training ground. I learned how to build teams, cast vision, steward people well, navigate conflict, and grow something fragile into something stable. More importantly, those years taught me how to carry responsibility without needing affirmation to keep going.

And then, when the assignment changed, I was free to say yes.

What once felt like being overlooked, I now see as God’s protection. He wasn’t withholding something good. He was making room for something bigger than I could see at the time.

That doesn’t mean the confusion or the disappointment was imaginary. It means it was incomplete. Meaning often comes later. Obedience rarely comes with full explanation. Sometimes God closes doors not because you’re unqualified but because He’s positioning you for work you can’t yet imagine.

God was doing His deepest work in me long before any institution was ready to name it. He trains us in obscurity, building character where there’s responsibility but no recognition. And when the time comes, the transition feels sudden to everyone else, but it’s anything but sudden for the person God has been preparing for years.

Those years in children’s ministry weren’t a detour or wasted effort, they were preparation. And the closed doors weren’t punishment, they were protection.

Once I saw it differently, I could finally let go of the need to understand why the church had been willing to trust me with so much responsibility but not fully recognize the role I was already carrying. I didn’t need that recognition anymore to tell me whether God had been using me or preparing me.

Looking back, the answer was already there. The ministry had stretched me, taught me how to lead, and given me experience I would later need. And because I had never stepped into that full-time position, I was still free when GiveSendGo came along. What had felt like rejection at the time had actually left room for the next thing God was preparing.

✦✦✦

Calling Comes Before Commissioning

When I began looking at Scripture through the lens of this lie, one woman kept coming to mind.

Deborah.

Her story is found in Judges 4, during a time when Israel desperately needed leadership. What has always stood out to me isn’t that she was a judge. It’s how casually Scripture introduces her.

“Deborah... was a prophet who was judging Israel at that time.” (Judges 4:4, NLT)

There’s no explanation of how she got there. No story about someone appointing her, or any ceremony to speak of. No public endorsement. Scripture simply tells us she was already leading.

Judges says she held court under a palm tree. Not in a temple or in a palace. She wasn’t in any recognized seat of power. Deborah sat beneath a tree in the ordinary rhythms of life, and people came to her because wisdom lived there. Her authority didn’t begin when someone handed her a title. It began when she obeyed what God had entrusted to her.

That challenged me.

For years, I believed leadership began when someone recognized it, offered the title, or officially welcomed you into the room. Deborah’s life dismantles that way of thinking.

She wasn’t waiting for permission.

She was already obeying.

One of the things I appreciate most about Deborah is that she never seems preoccupied with proving she belongs. When God tells her to call Barak, she does. She doesn’t second-guess her right to speak or soften what God told her to say. She simply tells him what the Lord has said.

She did this in a world where leadership was almost entirely male. Scripture never shows her fighting for the right to speak. It just shows her speaking, and the people coming to her.

That isn’t arrogance.

It’s confidence rooted in obedience. There’s a difference. Arrogance manufactures authority. Obedience simply trusts the One who gives it.

I think many women have confused those two things. We worry that stepping forward without someone else’s endorsement will look prideful, when in reality it may simply be the next act of obedience.

I spent years waiting for someone to tell me what God had already been showing me. I kept hoping another person would validate what He had already been growing in me.

Deborah reminds me that God’s calling doesn’t begin when people recognize it.

It begins when we respond to Him.

People may eventually notice.

They may never notice.

Neither changes what God has already asked us to do.

The lie of permission tells us to wait until the role feels sanctioned by someone else.

Deborah’s life tells a completely different story. Obey first. Steward what you have. Let recognition catch up later, if it ever even needs to show up at all.

Calling comes before commissioning. And sometimes the most courageous thing a woman can do is stop waiting for permission that God has already given.

✦✦✦

As I look back on those years now, I can see more clearly what I was actually wrestling with all along. It wasn’t my ability to lead, and it wasn’t even whether God had called me.

It was approval.

I wanted my faithfulness to be recognized by the same systems I was serving, and I was looking to people for permission instead of looking to God for clarity. More than anything, I wanted obedience to feel affirmed instead of lonely.

I understand that desire. We don’t just want to obey, we want our obedience to be understood. We don’t just want to lead, we want our leadership to be welcomed.

But Scripture names this tension with uncomfortable honesty.

Obviously, I’m not trying to win the approval of people, but of God. If pleasing people were my goal, I would not be Christ’s servant. (Galatians 1:10, NLT)

That verse doesn’t shame the desire to be seen. It exposes where authority truly comes from and draws a line between pleasing people and serving Christ. It just forces the question: whose approval am I actually living for?

Leading from obedience will feel unsettling in places where everyone expects permission to come first. When approval becomes the measuring stick, obedience can start to feel disruptive. And when obedience feels disruptive, the temptation is to delay it until it’s better received. But delayed obedience, when God has already spoken, is still disobedience.

I see now I was never lacking calling. I was waiting for permission that God never required. I had unconsciously decided that obedience would feel affirmed by the systems around me. If the right people didn’t recognize it, I assumed I must have misheard Him.

The closed doors weren’t proof that I was wrong. They were proof that God was shaping something in me that those spaces couldn’t yet hold. Some environments were never meant to validate what He was forming. They were meant to refine it.

And that misunderstanding kept me smaller than obedience required. It kept me cautious when I should have been courageous. It kept me waiting for a green light from people when God had already spoken clearly. I thought humility meant holding back until invited. I didn’t yet understand that sometimes humility looks like moving forward without applause, anchored in conviction instead of affirmation.

✦✦✦

The Truth That Replaces the Lie

The lie says I need permission before I obey. For years I acted as though people had to permit me before I could step into what God was asking me to do. But calling comes from God, not from people.

Leadership doesn’t begin when someone affirms you. It begins when you trust God’s direction more than you trust people’s approval. You don’t need to shrink your gifts or silence your voice until someone is ready to validate them. And you don’t have to wait until the timing feels safe or the recognition feels certain.

When God calls, He also carries the responsibility for what He asks of you.

Deborah didn’t wait to be invited into leadership. She was already leading. Her authority didn’t come from a title. It came from obedience. And that same pattern still holds today.

You may already be carrying responsibility and leading without anyone formally recognizing your position.

Deborah never asked whether she was permitted. She trusted the One who called her. And when you anchor yourself there, you’re finally free to lead without shrinking, without waiting, and without chasing approval from people who may never be the ones to give it.

For years I thought I needed someone to hand me the shirt. I thought I needed someone to say,

“Now you’re officially one of us.”

But God had already spoken and entrusted me with responsibility. He’d been preparing me all along. My calling was never waiting on someone else’s permission. It was waiting on my obedience.

Calling doesn’t begin when someone hands you the shirt.

It begins when you trust the One who called you.

And that is the truth that replaces the lie.

FROM A WOMAN WHO SHINES

By Krystal Parker

President and Founder, U.S. Christian Chamber of Commerce

I remember sitting in my car after a meeting, gripping the steering wheel longer than necessary because I knew what I had just heard should have discouraged me more than it did.

“It’s been tried before.”

“It won’t work.”

“Christian business leaders will never unify.”

“People are too territorial.”

Some people dismissed the vision politely. Others were far more direct. A few seemed almost amused by the idea that a national Christian chamber movement could exist in any meaningful way. At the time, there was no visible momentum to point to, no coalition of Christian chambers across the country, and no framework proving it could be done. There was only a quiet but persistent nudge from the Lord that would not leave me alone.

Start the U.S. Christian Chamber of Commerce.

The assignment did not arrive with applause or affirmation. Nobody handed me a title and said, “You are the right person for this.” Nobody opened a door and invited me into a ready-made movement. In fact, much of what surrounded the early days of the vision felt like resistance, skepticism, and even ridicule.

That tension became deeply personal because I understood why people questioned it. Logically, they were not wrong. The idea was massive. I had never built anything like it before. I did not come from a traditional chamber background. There were no guarantees, no investors standing in line, and no roadmap sitting on my desk. There was only an increasing sense that God was asking me to move before I felt fully qualified or fully affirmed.

What made it difficult was not simply the size of the vision. It was the temptation to wait for permission, funding, and approval before taking the first step.

I remember one conversation with my mom when I asked, “Is it wrong that I’m waiting on funding before I start this endeavor?”

She responded gently, “Would that be faith?”

She was right.

Faith is trusting even when you have no idea what the outcome will be.

So I began leaning more deeply into the Creator of the universe and the vision He had placed inside me. What I sensed repeatedly from the Lord was simple: take the next right step.

Not the full blueprint.

Not the ten-year strategy.

Just the next obedient step.

There were moments when building something without broad support felt painfully lonely, and I wondered if I had somehow confused ambition with assignment. I watched conversations stall, opportunities fade, resources tighten, and timelines stretch far longer than I thought they should.

One night after another discouraging stretch, I sat with my Bible open and landed on Isaiah 30:21:

“Your own ears will hear him. Right behind you a voice will say, ‘This is the way you should go,’ whether to the right or to the left.” (Isaiah 30:21, NLT)

That verse settled something deeply inside me because I realized God was never asking me to secure public consensus before obeying Him. He was asking me to listen closely enough to keep walking.

So that is what I did.

I kept taking meetings, building relationships, and inviting leaders into conversations about Kingdom commerce, cultural transformation, and the role Christian business leaders could play in shaping cities and communities. Some people understood it immediately. Many did not. Progress often felt painfully slow.

There were seasons where it seemed almost invisible, as though nothing meaningful was happening at all. But underneath the surface, God was building something I could not yet fully see.

Then gradually, almost suddenly, things began to shift.

Leaders from other states started reaching out, new chambers began forming, and conversations turned into partnerships. What once felt like an isolated vision slowly became a growing movement of Christian business leaders who believed faith and business were never meant to be separated.

Today, there are 35 Christian chambers—and growing—connected through our coalition across the United States, and over ninety percent of them are entirely new chambers leaning into the vision of Kingdom advancement through the marketplace, strengthening Christian businesses, and helping transform cities and communities through faith-driven leadership. Together, these chambers are serving thousands of Christian business leaders and building a network infrastructure for Kingdom business in one of our nation’s most critical mission fields: the marketplace.

Sometimes I stop and think about how easily fear could have delayed all of it.

If I had waited until everyone agreed, I would still be waiting.

If I had waited until the numbers made sense, I would still be waiting.

If I had waited until I felt ready, none of this would exist.

What God showed me through the process is that obedience often looks unreasonable before it looks fruitful. Many of the things He calls us to build will not make complete sense to the people around us at first because they are measuring them against current reality instead of future possibility.

I also learned that leadership rooted in calling requires a different source of confidence. Public approval rises and falls too quickly. Criticism can become deafening if you let it. But when you know God asked you to move, peace comes less from external validation and more from continued obedience.

That does not mean the journey has been easy. The journey came with real pressure: disappointments, financial strain, and exhaustion. But even in the uncertainty, I kept coming back to the same truth: God does not usually illuminate the entire path at once.

“Your word is a lamp to guide my feet and a light for my path.” (Psalm 119:105, NLT)

Not a floodlight for the next decade.

Just enough light for the next step.

That was the lesson all along. The Kingdom advances one act of obedience at a time, often long before the world recognizes what God is building.

And I am deeply grateful I did not wait for permission before saying yes.

Krystal Parker is the President and Founder of the U.S. Christian Chamber of Commerce and the Christian Chamber Coalition. She is the author of The Christian CEO and The Best Robot Wins, co-editor of Kingdom Commerce, and host of The Shepherd at Work radio show and podcast. A former Fortune 200 executive, marketplace strategist, and founder of Intent & Impact, Krystal is passionate about equipping Christian leaders to advance the Kingdom through business, leadership, innovation, and marketplace influence.
