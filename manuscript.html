BUILT TO SHINE

For the Woman Leading with Faith in the Business World

A Note Before We Begin

If you're a woman in leadership, there's a voice you know very well.

It does not show up at the loud moments. It does not interrupt you in the middle of a presentation or call you out in front of a room full of people. It is quieter than that. It shows up in the car on the way home from a hard meeting. It shows up at two in the morning when you are replaying a conversation you already finished hours ago. It shows up when someone praises you and you cannot quite take it in, and it shows up when someone overlooks you and you feel it more than you wish you did.

It is the voice that says things like, “who do you think you are?” The voice that says, “you should not still be here.” The voice that tells you to stop talking, soften your tone, hold back your opinion, smile a little more, take up a little less space. The voice that compares you to the woman across the table and quietly decides you are coming up short. The voice that asks you, after every hard conversation, if you handled it the right way and whether the people in the room are going to be fine.

That voice is not your conscience. It is not the Holy Spirit. It is not wisdom or humility or maturity. It is a lie. It is one of many.

Most of us have been carrying lies like this for years. We have absorbed them quietly, from the rooms we have led in, from the comments people have made, from the experiences we never quite processed. They have shaped how we lead, how we speak, how we make decisions, how we show up, and how we hide.

And because they have been with us for so long, we have stopped recognizing them as lies. They have been with us for so long that they no longer feel like lies. They feel like reality.

But they are not. They never have been.

This book is built around ten of them: Lies I have heard whispered to me, lived under, wrestled with, and slowly come to recognize. Lies I see in the lives of women I love, and ten lies I have watched hold back women who were called to do extraordinary things. Each chapter names one of them, walks you through what it sounds like, where it comes from, what it costs, and how it shows up in the rhythms of ordinary leadership. And then each chapter ends with the truth that replaces it. Not a slogan. Not a hashtag. The actual, anchoring, scriptural truth that takes the lie’s place when you finally see it for what it is.

This book is for the woman who is leading something.

A team. A company. A church. A nonprofit. A ministry. A family. A small business she built on her kitchen table. A board she sits on. A staff she manages. A house full of people who depend on her. She may or may not have a title. She may or may not be paid for what you do. She may or may not feel like leadership is the right word for it. If you are carrying responsibility for people, decisions, or outcomes, this book is for you.

It is also for the woman who quietly wonders if she is doing any of it right. The one who reads books with a highlighter looking for sentences that name what she has not been able to name herself. The one who loves Jesus and wants Him in the middle of her leadership, not tucked off to the side. The one who has been faithful for a long time and is starting to feel like something is keeping her from leading from a place of freedom, even when she cannot quite figure out what.

If that is you, you are not alone. And you are not crazy. There is a reason it has been so heavy.

You have been listening to lies.

There is no right way to read this book. You can read it straight through, chapter by chapter. You can flip to the lie that sounds the most familiar and start there. You can read one chapter a week and sit with it for a while before you pick up the next one. You can read it with friends, or you can read it alone in the quiet of an early morning when nobody else is awake. You can write in the margins. You can argue with me. You can underline the parts that feel like they were written for you.

What I would ask is just this, as you read, do not just absorb the words. Bring them to God. Let Him show you which of these lies has been speaking to you the longest. Let Him name what you have not been able to name. And then let Him do what He has always done, which is to replace lies with truth, fear with freedom, striving with rest, and performance with peace.

Every chapter is an invitation to lay one more lie down.

You were not built to lead from these lies.

You were Built to Shine!

Chapter One

The Lie of Permission

Lie: I need permission before I obey.

There is a particular kind of leadership a lot of women know very well.

You are running the thing without anyone ever saying you are running it. You are making the decisions. You are training the volunteers. You are answering the questions. You are smoothing the conflicts. You are the one people text when something needs to happen. And if you stopped showing up, the whole thing would probably fall apart inside a week.

But your name is not on anything. There is no title on your email signature. There is no salary attached to your work. You are not officially in charge.

So you keep going. You keep carrying it. And every now and then, you ask the question quietly to yourself or to your husband or to God:

Why am I doing all of this without permission to call it what it is? Why can I be trusted with the responsibility but not with the role?

That question is where this chapter starts—because it is where the lie starts. Somewhere along the way, a lot of us came to believe that we needed permission before we obeyed.

✦ ✦ ✦

I did not grow up lacking confidence.

From a young age, leadership felt natural to me, though I would not have called it that at the time. What I knew was that I liked being given responsibility. I liked being trusted. I liked being handed something to steward and figuring out how to do it well. I did not crave attention as much as I craved being useful. There was something deeply satisfying about being needed.

One of my earliest memories of this was sometime in middle school. I attended the children’s ministry at my church. One week I was asked if I would be interested in teaching an upcoming lesson. I still remember it clearly. The children’s pastor handed me a picture of a lightning bug with a short lesson and a Bible verse on the back about shining your light. I was told I could take it home, prepare what I wanted to say, and come back ready to teach the other kids.

I loved it! I was not overwhelmed and I was not nervous—I was excited! I spent the week preparing. I felt honored to have been asked, and not because I was given some kind of title or because anyone applauded me. I felt honored because I was given permission.

It may have been that everyone eventually got a turn; I honestly do not know. What I remember is not whether it was exclusive. What I remember is how it felt to be entrusted with something meaningful, to be recognized as someone who could handle the responsibility. A leader looked at me and believed I was capable of teaching, of leading, of contributing. That moment mattered enough that I can still remember it over thirty-five years later.

That experience affirmed something in me that I would carry for years. Leadership was not about age or authority. It was about stewardship. If you were faithful with what you were given, you could influence the space around you and more opportunities would open up.

As I moved into high school, that pattern continued. Our youth group had a student leadership team, and it was not just in name only. We were invited into real responsibility: we helped plan events, we discussed direction, we talked about culture and how our group functioned. The adult leaders listened to us. They expected something of us. They held us to a high standard. When we messed up, they corrected us and gave us trust and direction as we moved forward.

I learned how to show up. I learned how to serve well. I learned how to lead my peers with care. I learned that vision shapes people and that consistency builds trust. Leadership was not something I aspired to someday, it was something I was already practicing.

Those early experiences shaped me more than I realized at the time. They taught me how to plan and communicate clearly, how to take ownership of what I was given, and how to carry responsibility without waiting for someone to notice. I watched great leaders influence culture without demanding attention, and I saw how a whole environment could shift when one person quietly decided to take responsibility for it.

They also taught me something more subtle, something I wouldn’t name until years later. There was a clear, predictable rhythm: step up, be reliable, and add value. When you did, you were entrusted with more, and your voice carried weight. It felt empowering. IT was a pattern that for a long time, seemed not just right, but good.

I would not have agreed with any of that if you had asked me at the time, but it was forming the framework in my head: leadership happened when someone invited you. Influence grew when someone validated you. Belonging followed contribution. And I saw it play out around me, too. Once you stopped being useful, or stopped contributing the way the people in charge wanted, you could lose all of it. You could lose the invitation.

And obedience to God? Obedience followed the invitation. It followed someone seeing something in me first.

I did not realize any of that at the time. It felt healthy. But underneath it I was being trained to move when trusted and to wait or try harder when overlooked. I was learning to lead when called and to hesitate when silence followed. I was learning that permission preceded action.

That belief would follow me into rooms much bigger than a children’s ministry classroom.

When affirmation was present, I moved confidently. When it was absent, I paused. I assumed the pause meant something. I assumed silence was direction. If no one had asked, maybe it was not mine to step into. If no one had affirmed me, maybe it was not yet time to speak. If no one had opened the door, maybe it was not my role to even knock.

The lie did not begin as insecurity. It began as structure.

I had experienced the blessing of being entrusted. I had tasted the joy of responsibility. I had seen the fruit of faithfulness being recognized. The problem was that I began to equate affirmation with authority. If someone believed in me, I could move. If someone validated me, I was invited into action. If someone handed me responsibility, I stepped up and embraced it.

But what happened when no one saw me? When no one recognized what I had to offer? When the calling God placed on me was internal and not external? When conviction came before consensus? When obedience required me to step forward without being asked, without anyone else’s approval?

That is where the tension would eventually surface. And it always did. I have seen this pattern in my own life and in the lives of other women—too many times to count.

Because the Kingdom of God does not always operate on the same visible patterns as our worldly systems do. God does not always wait for someone else to validate what He has already spoken. Calling does not always come with applause. Sometimes it comes with discomfort. Sometimes it comes with resistance. Sometimes it comes quietly and asks you to move before anyone else understands why.

And if you have trained yourself to wait for permission from someone other than God, that moment can feel paralyzing.

Looking back, I can see that my early leadership experiences were gifts. They were formative. They built my competence, my confidence and my character. They taught me stewardship and responsibility, and I would not trade those years for anything.

But they also planted something in me that I did not see for a long time. Somewhere along the way, I began to confuse affirmation with authority, and that is where the lie began—the lie that I needed permission before I fully obeyed.

✦ ✦ ✦

That belief followed me right into adulthood.

After Dan and I got married, we made a conscious decision to find a church that would be our own—not one of the churches our families attended or any we had grown up in. We wanted something chosen together, not inherited. A place where our faith would deepen because we were investing in it together, not just continuing what was familiar.

We visited several churches in our area. Some left us encouraged and some left us unsure, and we would quietly process in the car afterward what felt aligned and what did not. We were looking for a place where we could grow, serve, and build real community. Not just attend, but belong. Eventually we found one that felt like a good fit, and we hopped right in. When the doors were open, we showed up. We built relationships, made friends quickly and deeply, joined a young married couples Sunday school class, and felt like we were part of the family there.

Before long, Dan was asked to help with music. He is a gifted musician and vocalist, and someone noticed. At the time, it was a simple ask: Would he be willing to lead a short song time before Sunday school? Just a few songs of praise and worship before people were dismissed to their classes. He said yes.

He showed up faithfully. Dan on the keyboard, leading people in worship. Over time that gift became clearer to other people too. He was mentored by a close friend, David, who was serving as the worship pastor at the time. His confidence grew. His calling sharpened. What started small slowly turned into something more defined, and eventually his leadership was recognized formally. So, he stepped out of his IT job, and into a worship pastor role.

At the same time, I found my place in the children’s ministry.

It was a natural fit for me. I came from a large family, the oldest girl in a family of twelve kids, and I had already served in children’s ministry at previous churches. When I saw a small children’s program at our new church, with fewer than ten kids and loosely structured with no real leader or direction, I did what I loved to do. I stepped in without hesitation and got to work.

I started teaching. I got to know the kids. I picked curriculums and built systems. I recruited and trained volunteers. I cast vision for the ministry. I cared deeply about the families and the spiritual growth of the kids in that ministry. And it grew. Teams expanded, parents engaged, and what had been fragile became stable. We built an amazing program with an enthusiastic, sold-out team of volunteers giving everything for the families of that church. VBS. Easter programs. Kids choir and mime group. It was exciting and it was growing.

I was not officially on staff, but I was functioning as the leader of the ministry.

I was in the meetings, shaping plans, fielding questions, and carrying responsibility when things needed attention. If a volunteer did not show up, it landed on my plate. If something needed vision, I was asked to provide input. There was no formal title attached to it, but the weight was real. And I carried it willingly. I was thriving and God was using me to build something new.

As the ministry grew, I was invited into staff meetings, especially when larger events were being planned. Easter. Christmas. Major initiatives where everyone needed to be aligned. I would sit at the table, contribute ideas, coordinate plans, and carry responsibility for how families and children would experience those moments. At the time I did not question any of it. I loved the work. I believed in what we were building. I felt trusted. In many ways I was honored to be in the room.

And then came the t-shirt.

The staff had t-shirts made for a campaign we were running. Everyone who was on staff received one. As they were handed out around the table, I realized they had not ordered me one.

I understood the logistics. I was not on payroll. My name was not technically on the staff list. It was simply how the order had been placed. There was no malice in it. No intentional exclusion. If I had asked for one, I am sure someone would have ordered one for me without hesitation.

And yet, the moment landed heavily on me that day.

I knew it was not really about the shirt. It was the realization that I was close enough to be included when work needed to be done. Close enough to be invited into planning meetings. Close enough to carry responsibility and represent the ministry publicly. But still held just outside the circle of formal belonging.

I stood there watching everyone else put theirs on, smiling and laughing, a team sold out to the mission, and the sting of being left out hit… hard. I was hurt. I was angry. There was a frustration that rose faster than I knew what to do with. For a brief moment I just wanted to quit. I wanted to step back and say, If I am not really part of this, then maybe I should stop carrying it like I am.

The thoughts came quickly, charged with emotion: Why am I giving so much if I am still on the outside? Why am I leading without being recognized as a leader? If I am trusted with responsibility, why am I not trusted with belonging?

None of it was about just cotton and ink. It was about what the shirt represented. It was a visible declaration . It said this is who is in. This is who we recognize as part of the team. I had assumed that I belonged. That I had permission to be part of the leadership staff at the church. But in that moment, it felt like I did not.

I still remember it today, all these years later, because it exposed something deeper in me. I had tied my willingness to carry responsibility to the hope that one day it would be formally acknowledged. I told myself I was serving purely. And in many ways, I was. I loved watching kids come to know Jesus. I was excited about seeing my leaders grow and flourish. But I did not realize that the framework underneath my leadership was a quiet expectation that faithfulness and hard work would eventually secure my place.

Standing there in that meeting, I realized how much I longed for something official. Something concrete. Something that said you are not just useful—you are chosen. You are recognized. You are inside the circle. You are one of us.

And when that visible marker did not come, it shook me, and I picked up that hurt and carried it.

It was such a small detail in the scope of ministry life. No one else likely remembers it. But for me, it marked a shift. It revealed how closely I had tied belonging to people’s affirmation. How much I believed that if I was truly meant to lead, someone would eventually make it official.

✦ ✦ ✦

Over and over again, this same pattern repeated itself at different churches.

Dan would step into a paid ministry role, and I would step into a volunteer leadership role in children’s ministry. I would pour myself into it, lead teams, cast vision, and shoulder the responsibility. And eventually I would ask what felt like a reasonable question: Could this be my official job? Could I have a title? Could I be paid? I am already doing the work.

The answer was always no. Always.

Sometimes it was framed gently. Other times it was blunt. We are not hiring for that role. We have more important positions to fill first. They would fill me with words of affirmation because they needed me, but the words started meaning less and less over time. Often, after I stepped out of the role, they would go on to hire someone for that same position.

The most confusing part was not the rejection. It was the contradiction. I was capable enough to carry the weight, trusted enough to fix the problems, and reliable enough to lead the volunteers. But I was not worthy of formal authority, compensation or investment.

I believed I was doing exactly what God had called me to do, and yet the door I expected to open, the one I wanted, the one I kept holding out hope for, never did.

There was one time when I decided I was going to ask for a title, without even the expectation of being paid.

At that point I had been helping build the fourth and fifth grade ministry at a church that was beginning to experience real growth. The children’s ministry had reached a point where age groups needed to be separated to serve kids more intentionally, and I was asked to take responsibility for the fourth and fifth graders. I was given a room to decorate with a budget to make it cool. I was given freedom to pick the curriculum and build a leadership team.

I stepped into that role with energy and clarity. Kids were inviting friends. Attendance was growing. We started something we called Club 45. Nights that were simply about fun, connection, and building community. We also created intentional discipleship time where we opened Scripture together and talked honestly about what faith looked like in everyday life.

A lot was happening. And I was thriving.

My own kids were getting older, and I knew that eventually I would move from being a stay at home mom and would need to step into work outside the home. As I looked ahead, it felt natural to ask whether the role I was already faithfully carrying could become my actual job.

I also deeply respected the children’s pastor I was working with. She was an incredible woman of God. She was wise, thoughtful, and supportive. I trusted her, so I went to her first.

I asked if I could step into the role of assistant children’s pastor. I told her I was not asking for compensation. Not a raise, (I was a volunteer) not a salary. I just wanted the title. I understood how systems worked, and I knew that if I ever pursued ministry work formally in the future, experience and titles would matter. I wanted my resume to reflect what I was already doing.

I told her I would continue doing the work I was doing and was willing to take on anything else a job description would require. She listened intently and told me she would need to bring the request to the pastor.

When the answer came back, it was no.

There was not much explanation. No clear reason given. I was told that there was another area in the church that needed help with growth, and that I could volunteer there if I wanted to continue developing.

I remember feeling genuinely confused.

I was not being corrected. I was not being redirected because of performance. Nothing about the ministry I was leading was being questioned. In fact, it was growing and thriving. At the time, I could not fully name what felt so unsettling about that conversation. Looking back now, I understand it clearly.

I was not being told I lacked ability. I was not being told I lacked faithfulness. I was not being told I lacked fruit.

I was being told I lacked human permission. And that distinction matters.

Looking back, I can see this wasn't an isolated moment. It was a pattern that repeated itself for years, quietly reinforcing the belief that someone else had to authorize what God had already placed in me.

✦ ✦ ✦

But over time, with distance and discernment, I came to understand that whole season differently.

I no longer wonder what those closed doors meant. I know now that God was shutting the door in children’s ministry, and not as a rejection but as preparation.

At the time, I could not see it. I only felt the confusion of fruit without affirmation. But looking back, I can see the kindness in those closed doors. If I had been serving as a full time children’s leader when the opportunity for GiveSendGo came along, I would have had to say no. I would have already been doing the work I loved most. My capacity would have been spoken for. Instead, God was quietly keeping me available.

Those years were not wasted. They were a training ground. I learned how to build teams, cast vision, steward people well, navigate conflict, and lead without titles or recognition. I learned how to grow something fragile into something stable. I learned how to carry responsibility without needing affirmation to keep going.

And then, when the assignment changed, I was free to say yes.

What once felt like being overlooked, I now see as God’s protection. He was not withholding something good. He was making room for something bigger than I could see at the time.

That does not mean the confusion or the disappointment was imaginary. It means it was incomplete. Meaning often comes later. Obedience rarely comes with full explanation. Sometimes God closes doors not because you are unqualified but because He is positioning you for work you cannot yet imagine.

God often does His deepest work in people long before institutions are ready to name it. He trains in obscurity. He builds character without platforms. He develops leaders in spaces where there is responsibility but no recognition and influence but no title. And when the time comes, the transition feels sudden to everyone else, but it is anything but sudden for the person who has been prepared quietly for years.

Those years in children’s ministry were not a detour. They were not wasted effort or misaligned ambition. They were preparation. And the closed doors were not punishment. They were protection.

Once I saw that, I could finally release the need to make sense of every decision that had been made by people. I no longer needed affirmation to validate what God had been forming. I could trust that if God was closing one door, it was because He was already preparing another.

✦ ✦ ✦

Calling Comes Before Commissioning

When I began looking at Scripture through the lens of this lie, one woman kept coming to mind.

Deborah.

Her story is found in Judges 4, during a time when Israel desperately needed leadership. What has always stood out to me isn't that she was a judge. It's how casually Scripture introduces her.

"Now Deborah... was leading Israel at that time." (Judges 4:4)

There is no explanation of how she got there. No story about someone appointing her. We do not see anyone appointing her, there is no ceremony to speak of. No public endorsement. Scripture simply tells us she was already leading.

Judges says she held court under a palm tree. Not in a temple. Not in a palace. Not in any recognized seat of power. She sat beneath a tree in the ordinary rhythms of life, and people came to her because wisdom lived there. Her authority did not begin when someone handed her a title. It began when she obeyed what God had entrusted to her.

That challenged me.

For years, I believed leadership began when someone recognized it. When someone offered the title. When someone officially welcomed you into the room. Deborah's life quietly dismantles that way of thinking.

She wasn't waiting for permission.

She was already obeying.

One of the things I appreciate most about Deborah is that she never seems preoccupied with proving she belongs. When God tells her to call Barak, she does. She doesn't apologize for speaking. She doesn't soften the message or wonder whether someone else would be better suited to deliver it. She simply tells him what the Lord has said.

That isn't arrogance.

It's confidence rooted in obedience. There's a difference. Arrogance manufactures authority. Obedience simply trusts the One who gives it.

I think many women have confused those two things. We worry that stepping forward without someone else's endorsement will look prideful, when in reality it may simply be the next act of obedience.

Looking back, I can see that I spent years waiting for someone to tell me what God had already been showing me. I kept hoping another person would validate what He had already been growing in me.

Deborah reminds me that God's calling doesn't begin when people recognize it.

It begins when we respond to Him.

People may eventually notice.

They may never notice.

Neither changes what God has already asked us to do.

The lie of permission tells us to wait until the role feels sanctioned by someone else.

Deborah’s life tells a completely different story. Obey first. Steward what you have. Let recognition catch up later, if it ever even needs to show up at all.

Calling comes before commissioning. And sometimes the most courageous thing a woman can do is stop waiting for permission that God has already given.

✦ ✦ ✦

As I look back on those years now, I can see more clearly what I was actually wrestling with all along. It wasn’t my ability to lead, and it wasn’t even whether God had called me.

It was approval.

I wanted my faithfulness to be recognized by the same systems I was serving. I wanted permission to come from people instead of clarity to come from God. I wanted obedience to feel affirmed instead of lonely.

There is something deeply human about that desire. We do not just want to obey, we want our obedience to be understood. We do not just want to lead, we want our leadership to be welcomed.

But Scripture names this tension with uncomfortable honesty.

For am I now seeking the approval of man, or of God? Or am I trying to please man? If I were still trying to please man, I would not be a servant of Christ. (Galatians 1:10)

That verse does not shame the desire to be seen. It simply exposes where authority truly comes from. It draws a line between pleasing people and serving Christ. It forces a decision about whose affirmation actually anchors us.

Leadership rooted in obedience will almost always feel unsettling in environments where permission is expected to come before calling. When approval becomes the measuring stick, obedience can start to feel disruptive. And when obedience feels disruptive, the temptation is to delay it until it is better received. But delayed obedience, when God has already spoken, is still disobedience.

Looking back, I can see now that I was never lacking calling. I was waiting for permission that God never required. I had unconsciously decided that obedience would feel affirmed by the systems around me. If the right people did not recognize it, I assumed I must have misheard Him.

The closed doors were not proof that I was wrong. They were proof that God was shaping something in me that those spaces could not yet hold. Some environments were never meant to validate what He was forming. They were meant to refine it.

And that misunderstanding kept me smaller than obedience required. It kept me cautious when I should have been courageous. It kept me waiting for a green light from people when God had already spoken clearly. I thought humility meant holding back until invited. I did not yet understand that sometimes humility looks like moving forward without applause, anchored not in affirmation but in conviction.

✦ ✦ ✦

The Truth That Replaces the Lie

The lie says I must be permitted by people before I am positioned. The truth says calling comes from God, not from people.

Leadership does not begin when someone affirms you. It begins when you trust God’s direction more than you trust people’s approval. You do not need to shrink your gifts until someone is ready to receive them. You do not need to silence your voice until someone validates it. You do not need to wait until the timing feels safe or the recognition feels certain. When God calls, He also carries the responsibility for what He asks of you.

Deborah did not wait to be invited into leadership. She was already leading. Her authority did not come from a title. It came from obedience. And that same pattern still holds today.

You may already be carrying responsibility. You may already be offering wisdom. You may already be leading without anyone allowing you to name it that way. That does not mean you are out of order. It may mean you are obedient.

Deborah never asked whether she was permitted. She trusted the One who called her. And when you anchor yourself there, you are finally free to lead without shrinking, without waiting, and without chasing approval from people who may never be the ones to give it.

For years I thought I needed someone to hand me the shirt. I thought I needed someone to say,

"Now you're officially one of us."

But God had already spoken. He had already entrusted me with responsibility. He had already been preparing me. My calling was never waiting on someone else's permission. It was waiting on my obedience.

Calling doesn't begin when someone hands you the shirt.

It begins when you trust the One who called you.

And that is the truth that replaces the lie.

FROM A WOMEN WHO SHINES:

The Lie of Permission

By Krystal Parker

I remember sitting in my car after a meeting, gripping the steering wheel longer than necessary because I knew what I had just heard should have discouraged me more than it did.

“It’s been tried before.”

“It won’t work.”

“Christian business leaders will never unify.”

“People are too territorial.”

Some people dismissed the vision politely. Others were far more direct. A few seemed almost amused by the idea that a national Christian chamber movement could exist in any meaningful way. At the time, there was no visible momentum to point to, no coalition of Christian chambers across the country, and no framework proving it could be done. There was only a quiet but persistent nudge from the Lord that would not leave me alone.

Start the U.S. Christian Chamber of Commerce.

The assignment did not arrive with applause or affirmation. Nobody handed me a title and said, “You are the right person for this.” Nobody opened a door and invited me into a ready-made movement. In fact, much of what surrounded the early days of the vision felt like resistance, skepticism, and even ridicule.

That tension became deeply personal because I understood why people questioned it. Logically, they were not wrong. The idea was massive. I had never built anything like it before. I did not come from a traditional chamber background. There were no guarantees, no investors standing in line, and no roadmap sitting on my desk. There was only an increasing sense that God was asking me to move before I felt fully qualified or fully affirmed.

What made it difficult was not simply the size of the vision. It was the temptation to wait for permission, funding, and approval before taking the first step.

I remember one conversation with my mom when I asked, “Is it wrong that I’m waiting on funding before I start this endeavor?”

She responded gently, “Would that be faith?”

She was right.

Faith is trusting even when you have no idea what the outcome will be.

So I began leaning more deeply into the Creator of the universe and the vision He had placed inside me. What I sensed repeatedly from the Lord was simple: take the next right step.

Not the full blueprint.

Not the ten-year strategy.

Just the next obedient step.

There were moments I questioned myself honestly before God. Moments where I wondered if I had somehow confused ambition with assignment. Moments where the weight of building something without broad support felt painfully lonely. I watched conversations stall, opportunities fade, resources tighten, and timelines stretch far longer than I thought they should.

One night after another discouraging stretch, I sat quietly with my Bible open and landed on Isaiah 30:21:

“Whether you turn to the right or to the left, your ears will hear a voice behind you saying, ‘This is the way; walk in it.’”

That verse settled something deeply inside me because I realized God was never asking me to secure public consensus before obeying Him. He was asking me to listen closely enough to keep walking.

So that is what I did.

I kept taking meetings. I kept building relationships. I kept inviting leaders into conversations about Kingdom commerce, cultural transformation, and the role Christian business leaders could play in shaping cities and communities. Some people understood it immediately. Many did not. Progress often felt painfully slow.

There were seasons where it seemed almost invisible, as though nothing meaningful was happening at all. But underneath the surface, God was building something I could not yet fully see.

Then gradually, almost suddenly, things began to shift.

Leaders from other states started reaching out. New chambers began forming. Conversations turned into partnerships. What once felt like an isolated vision slowly became a growing movement of Christian business leaders who believed faith and business were never meant to be separated.

Today, as of May 2026, there are more than 33 Christian chambers connected through our coalition across the United States, and over ninety percent of them are entirely new chambers leaning into the vision of Kingdom advancement through the marketplace, strengthening Christian businesses, and helping transform cities and communities through faith-driven leadership. Today, what once felt impossible has become a growing national movement of Christian chambers and business leaders across the nation.

Sometimes I stop and think about how easily fear could have delayed all of it.

If I had waited until everyone agreed, I would still be waiting.

If I had waited until the numbers made sense, I would still be waiting.

If I had waited until I felt fully prepared, fully validated, or fully qualified, none of this would exist.

What God showed me through the process is that obedience often looks unreasonable before it looks fruitful. Many of the things He calls us to build will not make complete sense to the people around us at first because they are measuring them against current reality instead of future possibility.

I also learned that leadership rooted in calling requires a different source of confidence. Public approval rises and falls too quickly. Criticism can become deafening if you let it. But when you know God asked you to move, peace comes less from external validation and more from continued obedience.

That does not mean the journey has been easy. There were real pressures, real disappointments, real financial strain, and real moments of exhaustion along the way. But even in the uncertainty, I kept coming back to the same truth: God does not usually illuminate the entire path at once.

“Your word is a lamp to my feet and a light to my path.”

Not a floodlight for the next decade.

Just enough light for the next step.

Looking back now, I realize that was the lesson all along. The Kingdom advances one act of obedience at a time, often long before the world recognizes what God is building.

And I am deeply grateful I did not wait for permission before saying yes.

The Lie of Scarcity

Lie: If I don’t take everything, I’ll lose everything.

Scarcity is sneaky. Most of the time it does not even look unhealthy. It looks responsible. It looks like being dependable and hard working and always willing to step in when needed. It sounds like saying yes because you feel like you should. It feels like carrying everything because somewhere deep down you are afraid that if you stop, things might fall apart.

A lot of women in leadership are exhausted not because they are doing the wrong things, but because fear quietly got mixed into all the right things.

Scarcity does not always mean you do not have enough. Sometimes it just means you do not trust there will be enough tomorrow. Enough money. Enough opportunity. Enough margin. Enough security to finally relax a little.

When scarcity starts driving your decisions, everything feels urgent. Saying no feels risky. Rest starts feeling irresponsible. Boundaries feel selfish. You start reacting instead of discerning, and after a while you do not even realize fear is running the show anymore, because it has dressed itself up to look like wisdom.

I think scarcity tends to hit responsible women the hardest. The women everyone counts on. The women who know how to make things work no matter what. The women who can stretch a dollar, solve problems, carry a family, and keep pushing forward even when they are exhausted.

Until one day they realize the very thing they built to feel safe has quietly become a cage.

When we lived in New Hampshire, Dan bought me a really nice camera because photography/ videography had always been something that would light something up in me creatively. I absolutely loved it. Then not long after he bought it, he lost his job.

So I returned the camera.

At the time it just felt like the responsible thing to do. We were job hunting, preparing to move, and I was pregnant with our fourth child. A nice camera felt like a luxury we probably did not need.

A few years later, after we moved to Maryland, this new thing called eBay started becoming popular. Nobody really understood it yet. It did not even feel like actual shopping. It felt more like some addicting internet game where people randomly bid on things.

One night I was scrolling and saw a camera that looked exactly like the one I had returned. It came with lenses, accessories, and everything else. In my mind it felt like an incredible deal.

Now to be clear, I truly was not planning to buy it. I was just looking, which every woman knows are usually famous last words.

Then I placed a bid.

Someone outbid me, so naturally I bid again.

Then they outbid me again, and somehow I became emotionally invested in winning this thing for absolutely no logical reason, except that I don’t like to lose. It made sense. It was a great deal, something we would call “girl math” now. In my head I kept justifying it. This was not reckless. This was strategic. I was saving money, obviously.

Then I won.

And the second I won, my stomach dropped. This was real money. I hadn’t spoken to Dan about it. Is eBay even legit?

Immediately my mind went into damage-control mode. I came up with a plan: I would wait to tell Dan about it until the camera arrived, because then I wouldn’t need to convince him that I wasn’t being scammed! Surely, once he saw what an incredible deal it was, he would completely understand my very rational decision-making process! (At least, that was the story I was telling myself.)

That same night Dan came home and told me he thought we were going to need a new car because ours had broken down on his way home from work and it did not look good.

My heart sank.

We definitely did not have money for both a car and a camera, and suddenly everything I had spent hours justifying looked completely different.

Then the phone rang. It was the company calling to confirm the order.

Dan answered, listened for a moment, and then handed me the phone. I awkwardly confirmed our address, hung up the phone, and tried to act like everything was normal. But as soon as the phone hit the cradle Dan asked, “Who was that?”

So I blurted out… “I did a bad thing.”

To this day our family still says that line whenever one of us spends more than we should. “I did a bad thing.” It became one of those running family jokes that somehow never gets old.

At the time I was not thinking about leadership lessons or scarcity or anything deep. I was just a mom who “accidentally” bought an expensive camera on the internet and then panicked about it afterward.

But a couple of weeks later the camera arrived, and every single piece worked perfectly. I remember standing there holding it and realizing this little impulsive purchase had put something back in my hands that I genuinely loved.

And somewhere in the middle of all of that chaos, a thought started forming.

Maybe I could actually do something with this.

I remember thinking very clearly that I would much rather take pictures and work for myself than go back to waitressing.

So I started.

I came up with the very professional business name Capture a Glimpse Photography, practiced on my kids and friends, and jumped in with almost no clue what I was doing.

At the time I was part of a MOPS group, Mothers of Preschoolers, and one morning I stood up in front of the room and announced that I was starting a photography business. If anybody wanted pictures of their kids, I would do them. I charged maybe twenty dollars a session. I most likely lost money.

But there is something powerful about saying a dream out loud in front of people, because suddenly it becomes real. I did not have credentials, a portfolio, or some polished business strategy. I simply started with what I had.

This was before YouTube tutorials and online courses for everything, so I learned mostly through trial and error. I took classes at the community college, taught myself Adobe, and slowly what started as family photos grew into senior portraits and eventually weddings.

And somewhere along the way, without even realizing it, scarcity started creeping into how I ran the business.

At first it sounded responsible. Take every client. Keep prices low. Work harder. Say yes more often. I told myself I was building experience and any amount of extra money earned was so needed, and part of that was true. But underneath it, fear was slowly becoming the thing driving my decisions.

I believed if I did not take every client, there might not be another one. If I charged more, people would disappear. If I said no to an opportunity, it might never come back around again.

Looking back now, I can see I was not really leading the business with discernment. Most of the time I was simply reacting out of fear and calling it wisdom.

And something interesting started happening over time. The clients paying the least often demanded the most. They questioned everything, needed endless reassurance, and seemed impossible to satisfy. I stayed incredibly busy and incredibly tired, yet still barely felt like I was getting ahead.

A couple of years later, my oldest daughter got engaged, and we started looking at wedding photographers. I noticed something almost immediately. Whenever I saw photographers charging very little, my first assumption was that they probably weren’t very experienced. I wouldn’t even stop to look at their portfolios or reach out for more information. Whether they realized it or not, their pricing communicated how much they valued their own experience.

And suddenly it hit me that I had been doing the exact same thing.

The very thing I thought was protecting me had actually been diminishing me the whole time. I was undervaluing my work because I was afraid—afraid there would not be enough clients, enough money, enough opportunities, or enough stability if I stopped saying yes to everything.

But when I finally raised my prices, something surprising happened: I made more money while working with fewer clients, the work improved, the relationships became healthier, and I became healthier too.

Photography taught me something I carried into every season after that. Not every open door is automatically from God simply because it is open. Sometimes discernment looks a lot less spiritual than people think it will. Sometimes it simply looks like learning how to say no without panicking afterward.

The Bible talks about scarcity a lot, even if it does not always use that exact word. One of the clearest pictures of it is the Israelites in the wilderness.

These people had literally watched God bring Egypt to its knees. They walked through the Red Sea on dry ground and witnessed miracle after miracle with their own eyes. And yet it did not take very long before fear started creeping back in. Because the wilderness felt uncertain. Egypt was terrible, but at least they knew what tomorrow was supposed to look like.

That is the strange thing about fear. Sometimes people will cling to what is familiar even when what is familiar was hurting them.

The Israelites started romanticizing Egypt. They remembered the food while conveniently forgetting the slavery. They talked about bread and meat while leaving out the oppression and exhaustion. And we do the same thing all the time. We go back to unhealthy environments because they feel predictable. We hold onto exhausting patterns because at least they feel familiar. We say yes to things God may not even be asking us to carry, because busy feels safer than uncertainty.

The years in the wilderness exposed something deeper happening in their hearts. They were not just wondering where food would come from— they were wondering whether God could actually be trusted tomorrow too.

So God provided manna. Every morning, they woke up and found it waiting for them on the ground. There was enough for the day. But God gave very specific instructions. Only gather what you need. Do not hoard it. Do not store extra away just in case. Trust that tomorrow’s provision will be there tomorrow.

And some people still could not do it. They gathered extra anyway, because fear always wants something visible to hold onto. They tried to control what felt uncertain, and by morning the manna they stored had spoiled.

The issue was never really food. It was trust. Their bodies had left Egypt, but their mindset had not fully left yet. Scarcity was still shaping how they thought, how they reacted, and how they viewed God.

A lot of us live that way too. You can love God deeply and still operate from fear. You can be successful and still quietly believe that if you stop hustling for one second, everything will fall apart. You can lead a company, a ministry, or a family and still carry this constant underlying fear that there will never quite be enough.

But manna was never meant to be stockpiled. It was meant to teach dependence. Daily trust. Daily surrender. Daily reliance on God instead of fear.

That is what God was doing in the wilderness all along. He was not abandoning His people. He was reshaping them. Before they could carry influence, responsibility, and inheritance well, they needed to learn how to live without fear controlling every decision they made.

And that lesson does not stay in the wilderness. It shows up in leadership, business, parenting, relationships, and every season where things feel uncertain and your instinct is to grab onto everything you can reach. Scarcity will always tell you to hold tighter. Faith slowly teaches you how to open your hands.

The story of the widow of Zarephath has always stood out to me, because it feels so honest about what it is like to carry responsibility while standing in uncertainty.

This woman was not sitting comfortably wondering whether God might come through someday. She was in the middle of a famine, trying to keep herself and her son alive while watching the last of her food disappear. When Elijah meets her, she is outside gathering sticks so she can make what she believes will be their final meal. She tells him plainly that all she has left is a little flour and a little oil, barely enough for one last piece of bread before everything runs out.

And then Elijah asks her for bread.

If you really slow down and picture that moment, it feels almost uncomfortable. He does not show up carrying groceries or offering solutions. He asks her to make something for him first, while she is still staring at nearly empty jars and trying to figure out how to feed her son.

What I love about her response is that she does not try to sound impressive. She does not hide the situation or pretend she has strong faith. She simply tells the truth. “This is all I have left. This was my plan. This is how bad things actually are.”

There is something deeply relatable about that kind of honesty, because most of us know what it feels like to smile publicly while privately wondering how things are going to work out.

Elijah tells her not to be afraid and he promises that the flour will not run out and the oil will not dry up until the famine ends. But the miracle does not happen before she moves. She still has to make the bread. She still has to pour the oil. She still has to trust before she sees anything change.

And that part feels very familiar to me, because there are seasons in leadership, business, motherhood, and life where obedience rarely feels dramatic or confident. Sometimes it just feels like taking the next step while still carrying questions you do not fully have answers for yet.

What I find beautiful about this story is that God does not suddenly overwhelm her with overflowing abundance. The miracle shows up quietly through consistency. Every day there is enough again. Enough flour for another meal. Enough oil for another day. And over time, that daily provision becomes its own kind of reassurance.

Many of us want God to remove uncertainty completely, because certainty feels safer. We want enough answers, enough margin, enough visible security that we no longer have to wrestle with fear. But often God seems far more interested in teaching us trust than in giving us total control over the outcome.

The widow obeyed before she saw how the story would unfold. She moved forward while the jars still looked almost empty, and that kind of obedience always requires vulnerability, because it means releasing the illusion that we can secure everything ourselves.

I think that is part of what makes this story linger with so many women. We know what it feels like to carry responsibility for other people while quietly wondering whether what we have is going to be enough. We know what it feels like to keep showing up, keep pouring out, keep serving, while still hoping God meets us there again tomorrow.

And somehow He does. Quietly. Consistently. Often not in excess, but in enough.

How a Woman Shifts Out of Scarcity

If scarcity were simply a bad habit, most women would have fixed it already. They would set better boundaries, stop overcommitting, raise their prices, and finally learn how to say no without spiraling afterward. But scarcity usually goes much deeper than behavior, which is why it can be so difficult to untangle. Most women did not wake up one day and decide to become anxious, overworked, or afraid of slowing down. Somewhere along the way, usually during a very real season of pressure, scarcity started feeling necessary.

For some women that happened in childhood, especially if life felt unpredictable or unstable growing up. For others it happened during the early years of marriage or motherhood, when there never seemed to be enough money, enough sleep, enough margin, or enough help. Some women learned it in ministry environments where being constantly available slowly became tied to being faithful. Others learned it in business while trying to prove themselves, terrified that if they turned down one opportunity, another might never come.

The hard part is that survival patterns rarely disappear automatically once the difficult season ends. The pressure may change while the mindset quietly stays the same. What once helped a woman survive can eventually become the thing exhausting her.

And because scarcity often disguises itself as responsibility, it can be difficult to recognize. A woman operating from scarcity usually does not look lazy or careless. She often looks incredibly capable. She works hard, responds quickly, carries more than she should, and keeps pushing herself long after her body and mind are asking her to slow down. Over time she becomes so used to functioning in urgency that peace itself starts to feel unfamiliar.

That is why simple advice like “just trust God more” rarely helps. Most women caught in scarcity already love God deeply. The issue usually is not rebellion. It is exhaustion mixed with old survival instincts that still feel emotionally true even when circumstances have changed.

For me, one of the biggest shifts came when I started learning how to pause before responding to things.

That sounds incredibly simple, but it was harder than I expected because scarcity hates pauses. Scarcity wants immediate action. It creates this internal pressure that says you need to answer right now, decide right now, secure the opportunity before it disappears. And if you have lived in survival mode for long enough, slowing down can actually feel irresponsible at first.

I started forcing myself to stop answering everything immediately. Instead of automatically saying yes, I learned to say things like, “Let me think about it,” or “I want to pray about that first,” or “Can I get back to you in a few days?” It sounds small, but creating even a little space between what I felt emotionally and what I actually chose began changing the way I led.

At first the pause felt uncomfortable. My mind would immediately start imagining worst-case scenarios. What if the opportunity disappeared? What if someone was disappointed? What if saying no closed doors permanently?

But over time something interesting started happening: I said no once, and the world did not fall apart. I held a boundary, and people survived. I rested, and somehow the business continued functioning. Slowly I started realizing how many of my decisions had been driven more by fear than discernment.

I also had to start paying attention to the stories I was telling myself. Scarcity constantly creates internal narratives that sound wise on the surface but are actually rooted in fear. Things like, You should probably say yes just in case. You cannot afford to miss this opportunity. If you disappoint people, they may never ask again.

The problem is that fear and wisdom can sound surprisingly similar if you are not paying attention. Fear is usually frantic underneath, even when it sounds practical. Wisdom feels steadier. Wisdom can tolerate uncertainty without panicking. Wisdom does not need to grasp at every open door.

One thing that helped me practically was looking backward before making decisions forward. Whenever fear started telling me everything depended on this one opportunity or this one outcome, I intentionally started remembering all the times God had already been faithful in ways I never could have predicted beforehand.

Sometimes provision came slowly. Sometimes it came unexpectedly. Sometimes it came through closed doors instead of open ones. But when I stopped long enough to remember, I realized my life was full of moments where God had carried me through seasons I once thought would break me.

And I think that is where a woman slowly starts shifting out of scarcity. Not because life suddenly becomes easy or predictable, but because her confidence becomes less connected to circumstances and more connected to trust in the faithfulness of God. She no longer treats every opportunity like it is her last chance. She stops scrambling to secure what she believes only she can provide for herself. She becomes more intentional, more grounded, and less reactive.

Over time her “yes” starts carrying more weight, because it is no longer automatic. It becomes thoughtful. Intentional. Rooted in peace instead of pressure.

That does not mean fear completely disappears forever. There will still be uncertain seasons and moments where old instincts try to return. But eventually there is a steadiness that begins developing in a woman who has watched God provide again and again. Not perfectly. Not always early. Not always the way she expected. But faithfully.

The Truth That Replaces the Lie

For a long time, I thought wisdom meant staying open to every opportunity. If a door opened, I assumed I was supposed to walk through it. If momentum slowed down, my instinct was to create more momentum. More ideas. More work. More opportunities. Somewhere deep down, I believed that if I stopped moving long enough to breathe, everything might start slipping away.

And for a season, that mindset can even look productive. It certainly did for me. I worked hard. I grew quickly. I became known as dependable, driven, and capable. But this is where so many of us get caught: constantly saying yes comes with a cost. You cannot carry everything well at the same time, no matter how capable you are.

One of the biggest shifts for me happened as GiveSendGo started growing. As the platform gained traction, more opportunities started showing up than we could realistically pursue. Partnerships, projects, expansions, ideas that all sounded good on paper. And that became the challenge, because the biggest distractions are usually not bad opportunities. They are good ones.

I remember realizing that if we said yes to everything, we would slowly lose clarity around the very thing we were actually called to build. Our energy would become scattered. Our focus would fracture. We would spend all of our time reacting instead of leading intentionally.

A phrase became part of the culture at GiveSendGo. Jacob and I would often tell our team, “We were not going to let ourselves bleed to death by a thousand paper cuts.” The danger usually was not one massive wrong decision. It was slowly draining our energy across too many good but unnecessary things, until we no longer had the focus to do the main assignment well.

That realization changed the questions I started asking myself. Instead of constantly asking, “Can I do this?” I started asking, “Am I actually supposed to carry this in this season?”

Those are very different questions. Because just because something is possible does not mean it is assigned to you. And just because an opportunity is good does not automatically mean it is yours to steward.

That does not remove responsibility or hard work. It simply changes where the pressure lives. I still work hard. I still plan carefully. I still want to steward opportunities well. But I no longer feel the same panic I used to feel anytime something uncertain appears in front of me. I am learning that leadership rooted in trust feels steadier than leadership rooted in survival.

That shift brought so much more peace into the way I lead.

For years I believed I had to grab every opportunity before it disappeared. God kept teaching me something different.

He has never asked me to gather tomorrow's manna. He's only asked me to trust Him for today's.

And when you really start believing that, your leadership changes. Your “yes” becomes more intentional. Your “no” becomes less apologetic. You stop chasing every possibility and start protecting what matters most.

It does not mean uncertain seasons disappear forever. There will still be moments where fear gets loud, opportunities feel tempting, and slowing down feels risky. But there is a steadiness that starts developing when you have watched God provide enough times to know that your future was never resting entirely on your ability to hustle harder.

I do not have to grasp at everything to be secure, because God is faithful enough to sustain what He has actually called me to carry.

And that is the truth that replaces the lie.

There have been a lot of moments in my leadership where I have left a room and replayed the entire conversation in my head long after everyone else moved on. I would think about what I said, how I said it, whether my tone was too direct or my emotion too visible. I have wondered more than once if I am the EGR person in the group. Extra Grace Required. The one people quietly brace themselves for. The one that makes people internally groan when I start speaking. That question has followed me for longer than I like to admit out loud.

I am wired with conviction. When I believe in something, it shows. My passion comes out. My face shows everything I am thinking. I care deeply. I do not approach leadership casually. But somewhere along the way, I began to question whether that intensity made people uncomfortable. And if it did, I wondered whether I was failing in some spiritual way. Was I too strong? Too passionate? Too firm? Was I loving well, or was I just overwhelming people?

This is where the lie of likability begins to take root.

It tells women that bold leadership will cost them belonging. That visible emotion will cost them credibility. That if they speak with strength, they will be labeled difficult, divisive, or too much. The lie is subtle because it disguises itself as humility. It sounds like wisdom. It feels like maturity. It tells us that the safest way to lead is to soften ourselves just enough so that no one feels disrupted.

For women of faith, this tension runs especially deep. We want to reflect Christ. We know Scripture calls us to gentleness and patience. So when someone reacts negatively to our leadership, we do not immediately examine the context. We examine ourselves. We ask whether we crossed a line. Whether our strength was pride in disguise. Whether our clarity was actually harshness.

Over time, this internal scrutiny can quietly reshape how we show up. We begin managing perception more than conviction. We edit ourselves mid-sentence. We soften statements that were not sharp to begin with. We monitor facial expressions in the room before finishing a thought. We tell ourselves we are just being wise and considerate.

Sometimes we are. But sometimes we are afraid—afraid that if we lead fully, we will lose approval. Afraid that if we stop managing how everyone feels about us, we will stop being welcomed in the room. Afraid that being strong and being loving cannot coexist.

And slowly, almost without noticing it, we begin trimming back parts of ourselves that were never sinful. They were just strong.

✦ ✦ ✦

When I returned to the small group the next week after the awkward conversation with the leader, she pulled me aside again. Same hallway. Same uncomfortable tone. And she said, “I need to apologize again, because last week I didn’t really mean it. I am still struggling with finding you annoying.”

Looking back now, the whole interaction feels almost surreal.

By that point I had already spent the week praying about it, reflecting on it, trying to genuinely search my own heart to see if there was something I needed to change. But standing there the second time, something in me shifted.

Instead of spiraling internally again, I felt surprisingly calm, and a little confused.

I remember responding, “You do not need to apologize again. If you do not like me, that is okay. That sounds like something you need to work through. I do not have a problem with you.”

That moment did not magically heal every insecurity I had, but I do think it was the first time I consciously realized I could not spend my life carrying responsibility for everyone else’s personal reactions to me.

Some people were going to find me too loud, too expressive, too intense, too opinionated, or just simply too much for their personality. That did not automatically mean I was doing something wrong.

But I also think something subtle began happening in me after that experience. I started editing myself.

Not dramatically. I did not suddenly become quiet or withdrawn. It was much more subtle than that. I would soften the way I said something strong. I would throw in humor after sharing an opinion just to ease the tension in the room. I became more aware of people’s reactions while I was talking. Part of me was always scanning to see how I was being received.

Over time, I can tell you, that gets exhausting, because you are never fully relaxed anymore. Part of you is trying to communicate clearly while another part of you is trying to manage everyone’s comfort at the same time.

I think a lot of women live there for years without realizing how much energy it takes.

Somewhere along the way, many women start believing leadership and likability have to constantly be negotiated with each other. We start assuming every negative reaction means we need to tone something down. We treat tension like proof we have done something wrong. We become so focused on keeping everyone comfortable that we slowly lose the freedom to speak naturally.

What I did not understand at nineteen is that not everyone who dislikes your personality is identifying a character flaw. Sometimes people are just uncomfortable with women who communicate differently than they do. Sometimes confidence, humor, passion, directness, or strong opinions make people uneasy, especially in women.

And if we are not careful, we start building our identity around avoiding that discomfort of others at all costs.

That is where the lie of likability becomes dangerous, because it slowly convinces women that being well-liked should be one of the highest goals in leadership. Without realizing it, we start measuring our effectiveness by how little friction we create instead of whether we are actually being faithful to who God created us to be.

Jesus loved people perfectly, and people still misunderstood Him constantly. Some people were drawn to Him, some people were challenged by Him, and some people walked away altogether. That did not change who He was.

✦ ✦ ✦

I have always been an emotional person. Tears come easily for me, often without warning and without logic. I am not necessarily sad. I am not hurt. Sometimes nothing particularly emotional is happening at all. And in leadership environments, especially professional ones, that can feel like a liability.

One moment stands out clearly. I was working at a restaurant and needed to talk to my boss, who was also a friend, about switching some hours. It was not a confrontational conversation. I knew it would be fine. There was no reason to feel nervous.

And yet, as I thought about walking into his office, I felt the familiar pressure building behind my face. The tightness that warned me of the tears just waiting to appear..

I went into the bathroom and looked at myself in the mirror, frustrated. What is wrong with you? There is nothing to cry about. You’re not upset. This is going to be a normal conversation.

I tried to reason my emotions away. Eventually, I took a breath and walked into the office.

Almost immediately, my lips started quivering and my eyes welled up. I remember blurting out, half apologizing and half annoyed, “I can’t believe I’m crying. This is so annoying. I’m sorry.”

My boss laughed and said, “It’s fine. I’m used to you crying.”

He meant it lightly. The conversation went fine. Nothing bad happened. But I went home that night deeply frustrated, not at the meeting, but at myself. I felt like I did not have control over my own emotions, and that annoyed and scared me. In leadership, control feels important. Composure feels like credibility.

So I called a counselor. Not because something had gone terribly wrong, but because I wanted to understand myself better. I wanted to know why my emotions surfaced in moments that did not seem to warrant them.

One of the simplest and most freeing things I learned was this, stop fighting the tears. The more I tried to suppress them, the longer they stayed. The more annoyed I became with myself, the more power they seemed to have. But when I acknowledged them, when I just said, “Hey, I might cry,” something shifted. Most of the time, the tears subsided quickly.

What I eventually realized is that my tears were not the problem. My war with them was.

Somewhere along the way, I had absorbed the belief that credibility required composure. That to lead well, I needed to appear steady at all times. That emotion, especially visible emotion, weakened authority. If I could just contain it, manage it, master it, I would be more legitimate.

But what I was really doing was fighting my wiring.

Counseling did not reveal that I was broken. It revealed that I was intense. I process externally. I feel deeply. When something matters to me, it registers in my body quickly. The more I tried to suppress that, the more dramatic it felt. The more I criticized myself for it, the more power it seemed to hold.

The problem was not emotion. The problem was shame.

I had quietly concluded that strength looked detached. That maturity looked unbothered. That leadership meant never letting anyone see what something cost you internally. And because of that belief, I was dividing myself. Half of me was leading the conversation. The other half was managing how I appeared while doing it.

When you are constantly editing your natural responses, you are never fully present. You are not just engaging the issue in front of you. You are scanning the room, analyzing reactions, calculating how you are being perceived. You are not just leading. You are performing.

And performance is a fragile way to lead.

What shifted for me was not a strategy to cry less. It was permission to stop fighting what was not sinful. Once I stopped panicking about tears, they lost their intensity. Once I stopped apologizing for being expressive, I could move through conversations without the added layer of self-criticism.

Emotion did not remove authority. It humanized it.

There is a difference between being ruled by emotion and being expressive with emotion. Being ruled by emotion means your reactions dictate your decisions. Being expressive means your emotions are present but not in charge. I did not need to erase my intensity. I needed to steward it.

That realization reframed the entire lie.

When I stopped fighting my wiring, my leadership became steadier, not weaker. I was no longer spending energy trying to appear unshakable. I was just anchored.

God does not sanctify us by sanding off our humanity. He refines our character, but He does not erase our wiring. What He created on purpose does not need to be hidden in order to be holy.

The point was never to learn how to cry less. The point was to stop believing that visible emotion disqualified me from leading well.

✦ ✦ ✦

The hardest part was rarely the conversation itself. It was what happened afterward.

After difficult conversations, especially high-stakes ones, my body would exit but my mind would remain. I would replay the interaction slowly, almost frame by frame. I would think about my tone, my phrasing, my facial expression. I would wonder whether I paused too long or not long enough. Whether I should have softened a sentence or delivered it differently.

What I noticed over time was that I was not primarily questioning the decision itself. I was questioning how I had been perceived. Had I crossed an invisible line? Had I sounded too strong? Too confident? Too firm? Had I unsettled someone in a way that would quietly follow me?

At first, I told myself this was self-awareness. That I was just being reflective. That this level of analysis meant I cared about leading well. But over time, I began to notice how much energy it was costing me. I would carry emotional residue long after the moment had passed. The decision was made. The issue was addressed. But I was still internally litigating my performance.

Eventually, I became curious enough to ask whether this mental loop was simply the price of leadership, or whether I was carrying a weight that leadership had never actually required of me.

I wanted to understand the anatomy of a leader’s mind. What happened internally after a hard conversation? Did healthy leaders replay every word, analyze their tone, and wonder whether they had been too direct, too strong, or simply too much?

So I asked Dan.

I described what happened after difficult conversations. The replaying. The analyzing. The wondering how I had been perceived. I asked whether he did the same thing.

His answer came quickly and simply.

No.

He might think about the substance of the conversation. If something had not landed well, he would make a note to adjust his approach next time. But he did not carry the conversation home. He did not relive it. He did not continue putting himself on trial long after the meeting had ended.

Then I asked Jacob.

I described the same pattern: the second-guessing, the mental loop, the lingering weight. I asked whether this was just a normal part of leadership, something every leader quietly carried.

His answer was the same.

No.

I was not surprised that Dan and Jacob, as men, might process leadership differently than I did. That was not the revelation. The revelation was that I had assumed this burden was necessary.

It was not.

I began to see how easily women can pick up responsibilities that were never actually assigned to us. We do not only take responsibility for the decision, the direction, or the outcome. We also take responsibility for how everyone feels about the decision, how they feel about us, and whether our leadership made anyone uncomfortable.

I had normalized something that was not necessary. What I had been calling responsibility was, in many ways, self-surveillance. I was not improving my leadership. I was policing my presence.

I was carrying responsibility not only for the outcome of the decision, but for everyone’s emotional experience of it.

Self-awareness is good. Reflecting on how we lead is healthy. But there is a line between reflection and rumination, and I had quietly crossed it. Reflection asks what you can learn and moves you forward. Rumination turns inward and starts questioning your identity. It shifts from “How can I improve?” to “Am I too much? Am I the problem?”

Reflection strengthens leadership. Rumination erodes it.

As I listened to Jacob and Dan describe their internal process, I realized they reflected when necessary, but they did not punish themselves for occupying space. They did not carry responsibility for everyone’s interpretation of their tone. They did not assume that discomfort automatically meant wrongdoing. They assessed, adjusted if needed, and moved on.

The lie of likability does not just shape how women lead in the room. It follows them home. It keeps them awake. It convinces them that every strong moment requires emotional repayment.

And unless it is named, it quietly trains women to shrink, not because they lack conviction, but because they are tired of carrying the invisible cost.

That was the moment I began to see clearly that self-surveillance is not humility. It is fear dressed up in spiritual language. And once I saw it, I could no longer pretend it was necessary.

✦ ✦ ✦

A Misunderstood Picture of Jesus

Part of why this lie holds so much power for faith-filled women is the way we often imagine Jesus.

We picture Him gentle. And He was. We picture Him compassionate, welcoming children, eating with outcasts, touching the untouchable. All of that is true. His kindness was radical. His mercy was consistent. His patience was remarkable.

But sometimes we reduce Him to a peaceful background presence. A soft voice. A non-disruptive Savior.

That is not the full picture.

Jesus was not only gentle, He was direct. He confronted hypocrisy publicly.

He rebuked religious leaders to their faces. He called out pride, exploitation, and spiritual manipulation without hesitation. When the Pharisees twisted the law to protect their power, He did not shrink back. He intensified the law. “You have heard it said... but I say to you.” He took the external standard and drove it inward, exposing motives, anger, lust, and hidden sin. That was not passive leadership. That was piercing clarity.

Even His teachings about turning the other cheek and going the extra mile have been misunderstood. In their cultural context, those were not instructions for becoming a doormat. They were subversive acts. Turning the other cheek forced an aggressor to strike differently, leveling social hierarchy. Carrying a Roman soldier’s pack an extra mile did not reinforce submission, it exposed injustice. Jesus was not teaching weakness. He was teaching strength under control, dignity that could not be stripped away by power.

Jesus was not crucified for being agreeable. He was crucified because He would not compromise truth.

Yet His confrontation was never ego-driven. He was never defensive. He did not argue to win. He spoke to reveal. He corrected to restore. He confronted from authority rooted in the Father, not insecurity rooted in reputation.

Strength and gentleness coexisted in Him without contradiction. He could weep over Jerusalem and rebuke its leaders in the same season. He could kneel to wash feet and stand to pronounce judgment. He embodied compassion without surrendering conviction.

Somewhere along the way, many women internalize the belief that Christlikeness means never unsettling anyone. To reflect Him well, we must minimize disruption. But Jesus did not measure His obedience by comfort in the room. He measured it by alignment with the Father.

Faithfulness made Him misunderstood. It made Him labeled. It made Him opposed. But it did not make Him wrong.

And if you are following Him, faithfulness may do the same for you.

✦ ✦ ✦

The Truth That Replaces the Lie

The lie of likability tells us that being approved of is evidence that we are leading well. But approval is not the measure of good leadership. Faithfulness is.

The measure of your leadership is not how comfortable everyone feels around you. It is not how rarely you disrupt. It is not whether you are universally liked. The measure of your leadership is whether you are aligned with what God has asked you to carry in this season.

Jesus did not lead to preserve approval. He led to fulfill assignment. And because of that, some walked away. Some misunderstood. Some opposed Him. His obedience was not validated by popularity. It was validated by faithfulness. That reframes everything.

You start asking a different question. Not “Did they like how I handled that?” but “Was I faithful in how I carried it?” That shift does not eliminate tension. It anchors you within it.

And practically, this changes how you lead. You speak when something needs to be said instead of rehearsing it privately for weeks. You address misalignment before resentment builds. You hold boundaries without overexplaining them. You release conversations once they are finished instead of replaying them in your mind.

You let obedience be enough.

There will still be people who misunderstand you. There will still be moments when your strength unsettles someone. But you no longer collapse inward at the first sign of discomfort. You evaluate honestly, adjust if necessary, and move forward without carrying emotional debt you do not owe.

You are not called to be universally liked. You are called to be faithful. And faithfulness will sometimes cost you approval. But it will never cost you alignment with God. When that truth settles in, the internal surveillance begins to quiet. The constant editing slows. You stop shrinking to remain acceptable. You stop assuming that leadership requires you to be smaller than you are.

Instead, you lead as a whole woman. Grounded. Clear. Honest. Steady. Not because everyone approves. But because you know Who you are accountable to.

God created me uniquely, and I am allowed to lead as myself.

And that is the truth that replaces the lie.

When I lead from conviction, grounded in Christ, the mission stays clear and people are served well.

Chapter Four

The Lie of Balance

Lie: If I’m doing this right, everything should feel balanced.

It is nine-thirty at night, and I am standing in my kitchen with a basket of laundry in one arm, my phone in the other, and a list in my head that will not stop scrolling.

Did I sign the permission slip?

Did I answer that email from yesterday?

Did I text my mom back?

The kids need lunches.

Is the meat thawed for tomorrow?

I never finished that one thing for work.

When was the last time I actually sat down with Dan that did not involve logistics?

I should be reading my Bible more.

I should be working out.

I should be drinking water.

And somewhere underneath all of that, a quieter voice.

Other women are doing this better than you.

If you are anything like me, you have stood in that kitchen. You have lived inside that scroll. You have had the moment where you wonder why everyone else's life seems to fit together while yours feels like a juggling act you are about to lose control of.

So you start chasing balance.

The lie of balance is a lie that follows women into leadership spaces, homes, ministries, and boardrooms alike. It does not arrive loudly. It does not feel rebellious or reckless. Like so many of the lies we carry, it disguises itself as wisdom.

If I were leading well, my life would feel balanced.

Not balanced in the way Scripture speaks about rhythm, rest, or obedience, but balanced in how we imagine it. Evenly distributed. Predictable. Manageable. A life where responsibilities stay in their proper lanes and the weight never tips too far in one direction. A life where leadership fits neatly alongside motherhood, marriage, friendships, faith, health, and work without friction or collision.

For faith-filled women in leadership, this lie carries an extra layer of moral pressure. When life feels overwhelming, we do not just feel tired. We assume something must be wrong with us. If leadership feels heavy, we do not first ask whether the assignment is demanding. We ask whether we missed God somewhere along the way.

We internalize the strain as a spiritual failure.

We chase balance not just as a practical goal, but as validation. Balance becomes evidence that we are competent, disciplined, mature, and faithful. When things feel chaotic or uneven, we interpret it as mismanagement rather than reality. We tell ourselves that if we were better leaders, better planners, better wives, better mothers, better Christians, this would not feel so hard.

But balance, as we define it through our twenty-first-century lens, was never promised. Scripture never presents leadership as tidy. It never equates obedience with manageability. And yet, many women carry an unspoken belief that if God truly called them, the weight would feel lighter, the demands would make sense, and life would fall into a sustainable rhythm that did not require so much sacrifice at once.

That belief is subtle. And it is powerful enough to keep us discouraged.

✦ ✦ ✦

There was a season when it genuinely felt like balance might finally be possible for me:

My kids were all finally in school during the week. After years of being a stay-at-home mom and working occasional nights and weekends in restaurants, my days had opened up in a way they never had before. There was margin. Real margin. Quiet hours. Space to think. Space to work. Space to imagine something outside the walls of our home.

I went looking for what I jokingly called a ‘real job’. I took a position at an arts council and then moved to a local marketing company working during school hours. I was using skills I enjoyed. I felt capable again. There was a sense of momentum building, like things were finally lining up.

At the same time, the idea for GiveSendGo began to take shape. Jacob, Emmalie and I were on the phone constantly. The conversations were exciting. Purposeful. Full of possibility. The vision felt aligned. It felt meaningful. It felt like stepping into something God had been quietly preparing long before I realized it.

Everything seemed to be moving in the same direction.

And then I found out I was pregnant.

✦ ✦ ✦

My first reaction was to cry. And if I am honest, I did not stop crying for most of the next nine months.

I was on the cusp of turning forty. My youngest child was ten. We already had four kids. Life had just started to feel easier. I had mentally and emotionally marked that transition, the closing of one season and the beginning of another. And suddenly, without warning or permission, I was staring down diaper bags, car seats, and starting over.

I did not want another baby.

That sentence still feels vulnerable to write, but it matters. Because faith does not eliminate disappointment. And obedience does not always arrive wrapped in joy. Sometimes it arrives with grief for the life you thought you were moving toward.

A normal day during that season looked like this. Go to work. Throw up multiple times. Feel physically awful. Feel very old. When they label your pregnancy a geriatric pregnancy, what can you expect? And still try to perform professionally.

Morning sickness for me was not occasional or limited to mornings. It was constant. Relentless. Draining.

On top of that, GiveSendGo was being built. Every night, after the workday ended, we spent hours on the phone talking through ideas, challenges, and next steps. There was vision and excitement, but there was also pressure. Decisions had to be made. Problems needed solving. Momentum mattered.

There was no balance to be found. Only endurance.

And beneath the exhaustion was something deeper: Confusion. This was not how I thought this would go. I had waited patiently for the right season. I had tried to align my work, my family, and my faith. And yet nothing about this felt manageable.

This is where the lie of balance does its quiet damage. Instead of acknowledging that some seasons are simply heavy, we turn inward. We assume the weight means something is wrong. We wonder if we misheard God. We question our capacity. We start negotiating with ourselves about what we should be able to handle.

Instead of asking what this season was requiring of me, I asked why I couldn’t handle it better.

✦ ✦ ✦

After baby Kenzie was born, who was the best unexpected gift God could have given us, I stepped away from my job to stay home. I assumed that would bring relief. I told myself that once I removed one major responsibility, everything would finally settle into something more manageable.

Instead, the demands just shifted. GiveSendGo continued to grow. It required full-time hours each week. Leadership did not pause just because other areas of life were demanding. It all just stacked, layered on top of five children, constant laundry, meals, schedules, and the invisible mental load that never truly turned off. The kind of work no one saw but everyone depended on.

Then one day, God asked us to say yes to something we never could have planned.

We welcomed five foster siblings into our home.

Ten. Nine. Eight. Six. Five.

Overnight, our family doubled.

Any illusion of balance disappeared completely.

There was no version of my life that could be described as evenly distributed or predictable anymore. Every area needed attention, often at the exact same time. Someone needed breakfast while someone else needed help with homework. One child was melting down while another needed a ride somewhere. GiveSendGo still needed leadership. Laundry never seemed to end. Meals had to appear three times a day. The invisible mental load only grew heavier.

That season taught me something I had resisted learning: life doesn't necessarily get easier as we move from one season to the next. It gets different. Every season brings new responsibilities, new pressures, and new opportunities to trust God.

The lie is believing that life will finally settle down "after this season." So we postpone joy. We delay rest. We keep telling ourselves relief is just around the corner.

But leadership doesn't move in straight lines. And obedience rarely waits until conditions feel ideal. Some seasons are not meant to feel balanced. They're meant to stretch us, refine us, and teach us dependence on God.

When we measure those seasons by manageability instead of faithfulness, we end up questioning ourselves instead of trusting the One who called us.

✦ ✦ ✦

Looking back, what made that season feel overwhelming was not just the workload. It was the weight of the expectations I placed on myself while carrying it.

There is a particular kind of pressure women carry that often goes unnamed. No one had sat me down and told me I needed to do everything well. No one explicitly demanded perfection. And yet I lived as though it was required.

I believed that if I were strong enough, organized enough, faithful enough, I should be able to manage it all without visible strain. I told myself that leadership meant holding things together quietly. That maturity looked like composure. That competence meant not letting the cracks show.

I'd scroll past women who seemed to have everything figured out. The successful businesswoman who also appeared endlessly present with her kids. The quiet morning with coffee, an open Bible, and perfect lighting. The color-coded calendar. The peaceful home. The organized life.

Without realizing it, I began comparing my behind-the-scenes to everyone else's highlight reel. I assumed they'd discovered something I hadn't. That balance was simply the reward for being more disciplined, more organized, or more spiritually mature than I was.

Comparison rarely announces itself as envy. More often, it sounds like self-criticism. I asked myself, “What is wrong with me that this feels so hard?” But the truth, when I slowed down enough to look at it honestly, was far simpler: we are all adjusting. We are all constrained by time. We are all choosing tradeoffs, whether we name them or not.

What I was comparing myself to was not reality. It was a curated snapshot. A filtered moment. A version of life that never included the unseen costs.

Balance is not something other women have unlocked. It is something we have all misunderstood.

✦ ✦ ✦

In leadership, the lie of balance rarely announces itself as chaos. More often, it disguises itself as discipline. It presents itself as high standards, as responsibility, as professionalism. It whispers that strong leaders hold everything together without visible strain.

So we absorb the tension instead of examining it. We apologize for being stretched, even when the stretch is legitimate. We minimize fatigue because admitting exhaustion feels unprofessional. We quietly decide that needing adjustment must mean we are falling short.

I remember a day when our home was full of foster children. A new friend from church, Jamie, who owned a massage business gifted me a session. I almost declined. It felt unnecessary. There was too much to do. Too many needs. Too many small people depending on me. Rest felt optional. Productivity felt essential.

But I went.

When I entered the massage room, everything slowed. The room was warm and softly lit. It smelled clean and calming. Worship music played gently in the background. For a moment, I felt my shoulders drop in a way they had not in months.

It was the first time in my life I fell asleep on a massage table.

I did not drift off gradually. I collapsed into sleep the moment my head settled. My body surrendered instantly. In that quiet room, without noise or interruption, my body told the truth my mouth had refused to say.

I was tired in a way I had not allowed myself to acknowledge.

I had been functioning. I had been leading. I had been mothering, serving, and building. From the outside I was showing up. But underneath the performance of steadiness, I was running on depletion.

Exhaustion often surfaces the moment we stop bracing ourselves. We may not give ourselves permission to say we are overwhelmed. We may convince ourselves that we are managing just fine. But when our bodies finally encounter stillness, they confess what our pride has concealed.

This is where the lie of balance quietly tightens its grip. But fatigue is not a failure. It is information.

Not every season is designed to feel evenly distributed. Some seasons require more. Some require surrender. Wisdom is not found in maintaining identical performance across every circumstance. It is found in discerning what faithfulness looks like right now.

✦ ✦ ✦

Eventually I realized I was asking the wrong question. Instead of asking how to balance everything better, I needed to ask something far more uncomfortable: what was I actually trying to balance, and why?

Not everything on my plate carried equal weight. Not everything I was juggling was ever assigned to me. And yet I treated every responsibility as equally sacred, equally urgent, equally mine.

My mom, Marnie, once shared a picture God gave her that has stayed with me for years.

In her picture, Jesus is hosting a massive celebration. The room is stunning. The energy is electric. And there is a long line of servants standing quietly along the back wall. Each of us has been trained. We know our role. We know our assignment.

The doors are about to open.

Jesus walks down the line, barely able to contain Himself, and stops in front of us. “I am so excited. My people are about to walk in, and this is going to be the best night. But I need you to listen carefully. Keep your eyes on Me the entire time. You cannot watch what Mother Teresa is wearing. You cannot track who Reinhard Bonnke is talking to. You cannot follow Billy Graham around the room. Keep your eyes on Me, because when I need you, I want your immediate attention.”

We all nod. Yes, Jesus. Of course.

The party begins. At different moments throughout the night, Jesus nods to someone in the line. That person steps forward and carries out exactly the task they were trained for. Quietly. Faithfully.

Then Jesus looks at me. He nods toward a table and back again. I know exactly what this means. I have been trained for this.

The dinner is over. My job is simple. Clear the table.

I grab my tray and begin removing plates and glasses. And then I notice something under the table. A napkin. Some green beans. A crumpled piece of paper.

My stomach tightens. This is Jesus’ party. It should be perfect.

I take my tray to the kitchen, but instead of returning to my place, I stop at the broom closet. I grab the industrial vacuum. I start weaving back through the crowd, quietly apologizing as I go.

“Excuse me. I’m so sorry. Just one moment.”

I ask people to move so I can plug it in. I turn it on, and suddenly the entire room fills with the loud roar of the vacuum. People stop talking. Heads turn. Eyes are on me.

I look up.

Jesus is standing right in front of me. He does not say a word. He just shakes His head. Barely. The smallest no.

I immediately turn it off, roll up the cord, return it to the closet, and hurry back to my place along the wall. The party continues and ends beautifully.

After everyone leaves, Jesus walks down the line affirming each servant. Then He stops in front of me.

“Marnie, you did a great job clearing the table,” He says. “Tell me about the vacuum.”

I start explaining. The mess. The importance. The pressure to make everything perfect.

He listens, then asks a simple question. “Were you trained for that? Did I ask you to step in?”

“Well... no. I was trained to clear the table.”

He nods. “I have people for under the table. They come later.”

If you want to serve Me, you must do what I ask. Not more. Not less. If I cannot trust you to stay within your assignment, you cannot stay in My lineup. I am not looking for servants who take on everything. I am looking for servants who obey.

✦ ✦ ✦

This is where balance breaks down for so many of us. We are faithful. We are capable. We are responsible. And slowly, quietly, we begin vacuuming under tables we were never asked to clean. We confuse overfunctioning with obedience.

I do not think God is disappointed in our desire to serve Him well. But He knows what is underneath that desire. He knows when our yes comes from love and obedience, and He knows when it comes from a need to prove that we are capable, valuable, or enough.

When I stop to think about it, my pursuit of balance has often had very little to do with wisdom. It has had everything to do with image. I want others to think I am strong, organized, and capable. I want to appear like I have it together. And somewhere deep down, there is a quiet belief that if I can just keep all the plates spinning long enough, no one will notice the areas where I am struggling.

Balance, in that sense, becomes performance with good intentions.

It becomes a way to manage perception instead of steward calling. A way to prove competence rather than practice discernment. And performance is exhausting. Because the goal is no longer faithfulness, the goal is concealment. Do not let anyone see the fatigue. Do not let anyone notice the cracks.

I remember countless times, especially when my kids were younger, driving home after dropping someone off or picking someone up, pulling into the driveway, turning the car off, and just sitting there. Radio off. No phone. No noise. Just stillness.

I did not plan it. I just could not move yet. I basked in the quiet.

Eventually someone would notice I had not come inside and come looking for me. And I would feel caught. As if rest required justification. As if the quiet needed to be explained.

Beneath that guilt was something harder to admit. Resentment. Not because others were failing me, but because I was holding expectations I had never voiced and standards no one else had agreed to. I expected myself to notice everything, manage everything, carry everything, and then quietly resented the weight of it.

I never asked for help. I never invited shared responsibility. I just kept going.

That is not balance. That is silent overfunctioning.

✦ ✦ ✦

This dynamic becomes even more complicated for women of faith. Because we do not just feel pressure to balance life well—we feel pressure to balance it faithfully.

I can look back at my early twenties and see this lie clearly now. I was involved in ministry. I loved God. I wanted to serve. And when opportunities came, I took them. All of them.

I dragged my kids to every church event. Every meeting. Every gathering. Nap times were sacrificed. Family rhythms were disrupted. Peace was consistently postponed. And when exhaustion surfaced, I did not interpret it as a signal to pause. I interpreted it as a test to push through.

The justification was always the same- I’m serving God.

That phrase carries enormous power. It silences questions. It shuts down discernment. It reframes overextension as obedience and exhaustion as faithfulness.

But service without discernment eventually costs something. Not because God demands too much, but because we take on things He never asked us to carry. There is a real difference between obedience and availability, and many women never pause long enough to examine it. We assume that if something is good, needed, or spiritual, it must be ours to do. We confuse opportunity with assignment. We confuse being capable with being called.

Over time, serving God becomes less about listening and more about proving. Less about faithfulness and more about output. And when burnout creeps in, we spiritualize it instead of addressing it.

God never asked for that. He asked for obedience.

✦ ✦ ✦

What Jesus Actually Modeled

One of the most disruptive realizations for me has been paying closer attention to how Jesus actually led.

Not the softened version we often imagine, the neatly packaged, always-available Savior who seemingly met every need placed in front of Him. But the real Jesus in Scripture, moving intentionally, withdrawing often, and leaving very real needs unmet.

Jesus lived in a world of constant urgency. Crowds followed Him everywhere. People pressed in from every direction. The sick wanted healing. The grieving wanted comfort. The curious wanted answers. Everywhere He went, need multiplied.

And yet Jesus did not respond to urgency the way we expect leaders to respond.

Again and again, Scripture tells us that when demand increased, He stepped away. When the crowds grew larger, He withdrew. When people wanted more access, more teaching, more miracles, He crossed to the other side of the lake or went up the mountain alone.

Jesus often withdrew to lonely places and prayed. (Luke 5:16)

That word often matters. This was not a one-time reset or a collapse under pressure. It was a pattern. Withdrawal was part of how Jesus led.

In Mark 1, after healing many and drawing massive crowds, people came searching for Him early in the morning. They told Him everyone was looking for Him. It sounds like an opportunity. A moment to capitalize on momentum.

But Jesus replied, “Let us go somewhere else, to the nearby villages, so I can preach there also. That is why I have come.” Needs were still present. Sick people were still waiting. Expectations were still unmet. And Jesus moved on anyway.

This is deeply unsettling if you believe that faithfulness means meeting every need you can see.

Jesus healed many, but not all. He taught crowds, but He also walked away from them. He loved deeply, but He did not live at the mercy of expectations. He disappointed people. He left questions unanswered. He allowed misunderstandings to remain. Not because He was indifferent, but because He was anchored.

In John 5, Jesus heals one man at the Pool of Bethesda, a place filled with sick and suffering people. He heals one and leaves. Scripture does not tell us why He chose that man and not the others. It simply shows us that Jesus did not feel compelled to fix everything He saw.

I can do nothing on my own. I judge only as I hear, and my judgment is just, because I seek not my own will but the will of Him who sent me. (John 5:30)

That matters profoundly for women in leadership. Because many of us live with an unspoken belief that if something is needed and we are capable, then we are responsible. If there is a gap, we should fill it. If there is a problem, we should solve it. If there is more work, we should carry it. And when we do not, guilt rushes in.

But Jesus shows us a different model of faithfulness. Obedience sometimes looks like restraint. Sometimes it sounds like saying no without explanation. It looks like stepping away even when people are disappointed. It looks like trusting that God is at work beyond what we personally carry.

Come with me by yourselves to a quiet place and get some rest. (Mark 6:31)

He said that not after everything was finished, but while the work was still pressing in. Jesus did not measure faithfulness by output. He measured it by alignment with the Father, and that invites a different question entirely: What if stepping away is not abandoning the call, but protecting it?

✦ ✦ ✦

Not long ago, I was sitting by the fireplace on a Saturday, reading a book. The house was quiet. There was laundry still to be done. My room was messy. There were emails I could have answered.

And I said aloud to Dan, “I feel like I’m being lazy.”

He looked at me and said, “You’re not lazy. You do a lot. You’re choosing rest.”

The accusation did not come from him. It came from inside me.

For many women, rest feels suspicious. It feels indulgent. It feels like opting out. We have been so conditioned to equate productivity with faithfulness that slowing down triggers guilt instead of peace.

But again, Scripture tells a different story. Rest is not a reward for getting everything done. It is an act of trust. It is a declaration that God sustains what we step away from. That the world does not collapse when we pause. That our worth is not tied to constant output.

God did not rest on the seventh day because He was tired. He rested because rest establishes rhythm. Jesus withdrew not because He lacked compassion, but because intimacy with the Father required space.

Rest is not laziness. It is obedience. It’s a commandment.

✦ ✦ ✦

The Truth That Replaces the Lie

The lie says that if you're doing what God wants, your life should feel balanced. If your life doesn't feel balanced, then something must be wrong with you.

But God never asked me to live a perfectly balanced life.

He asked me to faithfully live the life He gave me.

Some seasons are naturally heavier than others. I lived in some seasons that asked for more sacrifice. Some ask more of us than we ever thought we could give. That doesn't mean we've missed God. Sometimes it means we're exactly where He wants us.

I spent years believing that if I could just organize my time better, become more disciplined, or work a little harder, I could finally make everything fit. But every time one area of my life settled down, another demanded more of me. I finally realized the problem wasn't that I couldn't find balance—the problem was that I was chasing something God never promised.

I still catch myself reaching for the vacuum.

I still notice things that need to be done.

I still assume that because I can do something, I probably should.

Then I remember the story:

"I have people for under the table."

Every time I remember that picture, I breathe a little easier. I don't have to carry every need I see. I don't have to solve every problem or say yes to every opportunity. I don't have to prove my faithfulness by exhausting myself.

My job isn't to do everything.

My job is to faithfully do what He asked me to do.

When I stop measuring my life by balance and start measuring it by obedience, something changes. I no longer carry the pressure of trying to keep every plate spinning. I simply ask, "Jesus, what have You asked me to carry today?"

That question brings freedom.

Because some things belong in my hands.

And some things belong in His.

That's where peace lives.

And that is the truth that replaces the lie.

"Hi, Bud. Did you do your chores? Did you do your homework?"

Soren got what was left. Which was logistics.

But I had decided that good mothering meant I had to do all of the good things, all the time, regardless of what else I was carrying.

But love is the only thing that actually works that way.

For a while, the speed looked like strength. The meals were still on the table. The reports were still on time. The kids were fed. The firm was growing.

I didn't hear it happening. I didn't feel myself leaving. I just kept saying yes to every good thing until there was nothing left for any of them.

"Jenn, you are diminishing."

Some days my inbox is a disaster. I don’t look for perfection in all things any longer. Just excellence in the few things that matter.

BUILT TO SHINE

For the Woman Leading with Faith in the Business World

Chapter Five

The Lie of Control

Lie: If I don’t hold it all together, everything will fall apart.

I've never thought of myself as a controlling person.

In fact, if you asked the people closest to me to describe me, I don't think "controlling" would even make the list.

Driven? Sure. Visionary? Maybe. Busy? Definitely. But controlling? Probably not.

At least...that's what I would have said.

Looking back, I realize I wasn't controlling in the way I imagined controlling people acted. I wasn't trying to dominate people. I was trying to help.

And that's exactly why this lie is so deceptive.

Because most of us don't recognize control when it's wearing the clothes of responsibility.

For me, it usually sounds like four simple words:

"I'll just do it."

They sound productive. Responsible. Helpful.

But over the years, I've realized those four little words often reveal something deeper. They reveal how much I like being in control. What's funny is that I didn't always know that about myself.

A while ago I found an old VHS tape I had recorded as a teenager…Let me back up, when I was a teenager, I used to record “shows” for my camp friends, I would be walking around the house interviewing my siblings, showing my daily life and spending way too long flipping my hair back and forth as I inserted the word “like” like a valley girl.

On one particular tape, my brother was the one holding the camera. I started to talk to him, or maybe lecturing him about the camera. I was wanting him to “do it right”

"Hold the camera like this."

"No...not there."

"Turn this way."

"Do it again."

Finally I reached for the camera and said,

"Just give it to me."

Hearing my sixteen-year-old voice made me cringe and want to turn off the video... I sounded so bossy. Almost angry. So incredibly sure of myself. I was directing every scene as if the camera belonged to me, telling everyone what to say and where to stand. I didn’t think I was being controlling at the time. I thought I was being capable. I thought I was just the one who knew how things should go.

That's when I realized something:

Control didn't begin with fear.

It began with confidence.

When I sat down to write this chapter, I almost tucked it right into the one chapter about Balance right before it.

Balance and control are close neighbors. They live so near each other that it is often hard to see where the fence line is. We assume that control is the tool we use to build balance—that if we can just manage the details, stay ahead of the problems, and keep a watchful eye on every moving part, eventually everything will settle into a nice, even rhythm.

But the more I sat with God on this, the more I realized they are two different battles. Balance is about how we distribute the weight. Control is about who owns the outcome. And most of us cross the line from one to the other without even realizing we have moved.

I remember leading one of our first women’s retreats a few years ago. We were using Shannon Popkin’s book, Control Girl. She walks through the lives of women in Scripture, showing how control isn’t usually about being a rebel. It is about self-protection. She describes it as the way we live as if we are the ones in charge, even when we say we trust God, because letting go feels far too risky.

What hit me was how familiar that sounded. Control is rarely about power. It is about safety. It is the wall we build to keep out disappointment, chaos, or pain. Instead of trusting God with how the story ends, we quietly take responsibility for managing every scene ourselves.

For capable women, especially those in leadership, control feels responsible. Our experience reinforces it. We see that when we step in, things get better. When we anticipate a need, a fire is put out. Staying alert keeps the world from spinning off its axis. So we watch closer and step in more often, until we are carrying weight we were never meant to shoulder.

Control is not born out of arrogance. It is born out of pressure.

It shows up in the big things like micromanaging your team or refusing to delegate. But it also shows up in the quiet, ordinary rhythms. The frustration when the dishwasher is loaded “wrong.” The inability to let a team member do a job differently than you would. The reluctance to give people the space to fail so they can actually learn.

And that is where the lie lives: if I don’t hold it all together, everything will fall apart.

Control itself is not the problem—misplaced control is. It becomes destructive when it reaches past our responsibility and starts claiming ownership over people and outcomes that belong to God. It turns leadership into a heavy pressure. And for many of us, this pattern has gone unchecked for years because it looks like competence and it gets praised by everyone around us.

But what feels like faithfulness can quietly train us to live as though everything depends on us.

✦ ✦ ✦

For me, this started a long time ago.

I grew up in a house with twelve kids—six boys and six girls. My parents had their first eight kids in under eight years. Life was loud, fast, and full of motion, so we had to have structure just to survive.

Everyone had a chore or two. I washed the dishes every other night, alternating with my older brother. I also had to fold the laundry—the huge mound of laundry. I would fold it all and put it into everyone’s piles or baskets.

To this day, laundry and dishes are still my least favorite chores.

(And yes...I still think there's a right way to fold towels. Wait...is that controlling?)

Everyone in my family had a role. My childhood was wonderful, shaped by parents who loved us and loved Jesus. This isn’t a story of a hard life; it is a story of how I was formed.

In a family that size, you learn that noticing what needs to be done is a way of life. Carrying your weight keeps the whole ship moving. As the oldest girl, I was trusted with decisions and helped manage my younger siblings. I carried weight, and I liked the influence that came with it. Over time, responsibility started feeling like authority. Control didn’t feel selfish. It felt necessary. And because it felt necessary, I never questioned it.

I knew how to manage a room, and that instinct served me well. But it also planted a seed. Control takes root in dependability. We learn that things work when we step in, so we step in faster. We catch what others miss, until we can no longer tell the difference between faithfulness and management. But underneath it all, we aren’t protecting order. We are protecting ourselves. We are protecting our sense of being useful. We are guarding against the discomfort of letting go.

✦ ✦ ✦

For most women I know, the struggle with delegation isn’t about competence. It is about trust. We are afraid that if we let go, the ball will be dropped. And maybe it will. We have all cleaned up others messes before, so we step in to keep things moving. I saw this recently at GiveSendGo when we were hiring for our marketing team. Marketing was my world in the early days. When things felt unfinished or slow, I felt justified jumping back into the lane.

I saw that our social media channels felt like they were not getting the attention they deserved, and I knew the team was smaller at the moment so I decided to help. Soon I was editing, posting, and injecting my voice to help the team. We couldn’t afford to fail. The pressure wasn't imaginary. If GiveSendGo failed, there were real consequences. Employees would lose jobs. Campaigns would lose a platform. Families depending on us would be affected. The responsibility was real.

But somewhere along the way, I stopped simply leading what God had entrusted to me. I started acting like the outcome rested on my shoulders. I stopped carrying what God had given me to steward and started carrying what only He could sustain.

Finally, our new marketing director pulled me aside during one of our weekly meetings when I was sharing with him some of my new posts. He was respectful when he asked, “Do you want me to lead this, or are you going to lead this?” I felt a little gut punch because I knew it was true but I also had all the comebacks to defend why I was doing it. Instead, I took a breath and acknowledged he was right. I shared with him what I needed to see so that I didn't feel like we were dropping the ball, but acknowledged that me just jumping in was not the best way to help.

My “help” was actually a lack of trust. My fear of things not being perfect was keeping other leaders from growing.

Delegation requires surrender. Not just of tasks, but of outcomes.

Moses lived this tension. He was leading a massive, grumbling crowd and hearing directly from God. People stood in line from morning until night just to have him settle their disputes. It is easy to see why he stayed in the middle of it. He felt responsible for them, and somewhere along the way, he began to believe he was the only one who could do it.

But his father-in-law, Jethro, saw the truth: “What you are doing is not good. You will surely wear yourself out.” Moses wasn’t failing; he was overfunctioning. God’s answer wasn’t for Moses to work harder. It was shared leadership.

What feels like faithfulness can quietly train us to live as though everything depends on us. And when we carry that misplaced responsibility, the first thing we lose is our peace. The fruit of control is always anxiety.

✦ ✦ ✦

When Control Turns Into Anxiety

Control promises us safety, but it only ever hands us anxiety. I see so many faithful women in leadership who are just exhausted. They can’t sleep. Their minds are constantly racing. They stay tense even when they are supposed to be resting. Many of them love Jesus deeply, and yet they are carrying a weight that is crushing them.

That disconnect is real. Anxiety doesn’t come because you care too much. It comes because you are carrying too much.

When we try to manage the outcome, our minds stay on high alert. We are rehearsing conversations and planning for “what ifs” that may never happen. We are scanning the horizon for the next disaster. We are busy, but we aren’t peaceful.

“Do not be anxious about your life.” (Matthew 6:25)

Jesus didn’t say that because life is easy. He said it because anxiety shows us where our trust has shifted. It promises control, but it only proves how powerless we really are. Over time, it even changes how we pray. We ask for God’s help, but we keep a tight grip on our own plans. We want His blessing on our decisions, but we don’t want Him to interrupt them.

“Do not be anxious about anything, but in everything, by prayer and petition, with thanksgiving, present your requests to God.” (Philippians 4:6)

The invitation is to transfer the responsibility. The peace of God doesn’t come because we managed things better. It comes when we finally lay the burden down.I had always read this verse as a list.

Pray.

Petition.

Then remember to be thankful.

But that's not what Paul says.

He says to pray with thanksgiving.

Thanksgiving isn't the dessert after you've finished praying.

It's the posture you bring into the prayer.

It’s not separate things to be thankful for, but actually being thankful about the hard things you are praying and petitioning for. When you start being thankful in the hard season, you change your mindset and are able to release control. When we pray and petition WITH thanksgiving it actually changes things.

“Cast all your anxiety on Him because He cares for you.” (1 Peter 5:7)

You can only cast something if you let go of it. Control tries to treat God like a consultant rather than a Shepherd. But Jesus offers a different way.

“Peace I leave with you; My peace I give to you. Not as the world gives do I give to you. Let not your hearts be troubled, neither let them be afraid.” (John 14:27)

Letting go is not irresponsibility. It is the courageous decision to believe that God is at work, while acknowledging that we are ultimately not the ones holding everything together.

✦ ✦ ✦

At the root of control is fear. Fear that if we loosen our grip, things will crumble. Fear that if we stop overfunctioning, people will see we aren’t as strong as they thought. For many of us, that fear was born in a season when things really did depend on us, and control became our way of surviving.

I remember this from my early years in children’s ministry. I was young and I wanted order in my classroom. Every week the kids would be mid-lesson and someone would “have to go to the bathroom”. I put it in quotes because most of the time they just wanted an excuse to leave the room for a few minutes. Soon the next kid would need to go, then the next, disrupting the whole class. I didn’t want the kids to run all over me. So I made a rule: everyone had to go to the bathroom before class started. No interruptions. No wandering. No loss of authority.

One day, with fifteen minutes left in class, a little girl raised her hand. She had to go. I looked at the clock and told her she could hold it. I didn’t ask any further questions. I didn’t try to gauge this situation, I was the boss of the room and we waited until the end to use the bathroom. That wasn’t leadership. It was control. I was more concerned with my structure than I was with the person in front of me. A few minutes later, she wet her pants.

I was mortified.

I'll be honest. I almost left this story out of the book.

It's not exactly the kind of leadership story you hope people remember you for.

But if I'm asking you to examine the places where control has quietly taken root in your life, I have to be willing to show you where it took root in mine.

I can still remember the room going quiet. Her embarrassment was heartbreaking. Then I had to do the part I dreaded most as a twenty year old: explain to her parents why their little girl had wet her pants in my classroom.

In that moment, my fear of losing control had overridden my compassion. I was guarding my rules. I was guarding my "order." And a child paid the price.

Control promises to protect us. But when protecting ourselves becomes the priority, someone else usually pays the price.

God knows why we reach for control. He knows the environments that taught us to stay alert and manage closely. But He still invites us to release it, because control driven by fear always costs someone something. It costs us our peace, and it can cost others their dignity, confidence, or trust. It turns leadership into something much heavier than it was ever meant to be.

✦ ✦ ✦

The Truth That Replaces the Lie

The lie says, "If I don't hold it all together, everything will fall apart."

And it sounds responsible. It sounds mature. It even sounds faithful. After all, leaders carry responsibility. They solve problems. They protect people. They make hard decisions. But somewhere along the way, responsibility can quietly become ownership. We begin carrying outcomes that were never ours to carry.

The truth tells a completely different story.

God never asked me to hold everything together. He asked me to be faithful.

There is a difference.

Faithfulness means I steward what God has placed in my hands. Control tries to take ownership of what has always belonged in His. Faithfulness works diligently and then rests. Control works relentlessly because it believes everything depends on us.

When I live as though everything depends on me, I slowly stop depending on Him. My prayers become backup plans instead of surrender. My leadership becomes driven by pressure instead of peace. I become exhausted trying to carry a burden God never intended me to bear.

Real leadership isn't sustained by constant vigilance. It is sustained by trust.

Trust doesn't mean I stop caring. It means I stop believing I am indispensable. It means I can lead wholeheartedly without believing I am the one holding the world together. It means I can release outcomes into God's hands while remaining fully present in the work He has given me today.

So now, when I feel the urge to jump in, fix, rescue, or take over, I try to ask a different question:

Is this mine to steward, or am I trying to own what belongs to God?

That question has brought more peace into my leadership than almost anything else. Because leadership becomes lighter when I remember that I am not the Savior.

Jesus already has that job.

I am simply called to follow Him faithfully.

I am responsible for my obedience, not the outcome.

That is the truth that replaces the lie.

BUILT TO SHINE

Chapter Six

The Lie of Legitimacy

Lie: I’m not qualified enough to lead here.

Can I tell you something embarrassing?

Every single time we have people coming over, I clean my house like a maniac. Stuffing things in closets. Wiping down counters that were fine. Lighting a candle so it smells like I have my life together. And the whole time I am doing it, Dan walks through helping and says something like, “How is our house such a disaster.”

And I always say the same thing back.

“Babe, everyone cleans their house before people come over. That is the whole game. We all pretend we are organized, put-together people, and then the second the door closes behind them, we go right back to leaving shoes in the hallway and dishes in the sink.”

I think about that a lot because that is exactly what leadership can feel like.

Everyone walks in looking polished. Everyone sounds like they know what they are doing. Everyone seems to have read the memo on how to show up. And you are standing there, fully aware that you Febrezed your whole personality on the drive over, hoping nobody notices the chaos you stuffed in the closet.

That is imposter syndrome. And every woman I know in leadership has felt it.

What if they find out I don’t actually belong here?

What if everyone else really is smarter, more polished, more qualified, and I’ve just been faking it really well?

What if today is the day they figure it out?

This is not shallow insecurity—it is what happens when calling shows up before confidence does. When the responsibility lands before you feel ready to carry it. When you realize, mid-step, that leadership is going to ask more of you than you knew how to give.

Here is the thing about this lie: It usually does not stop us from stepping forward. We say yes. We show up. We do the thing.

What it does is stop us from standing firmly once we get there. It plants hesitation where there was clarity. It turns leadership into something we feel we have to justify instead of steward. And it keeps us slightly off-balance, always wondering if today is the day someone figures out we do not actually belong.

The enemy is sneaky like that. He rarely whispers, “You shouldn’t have said yes.”

What he whispers is, “You shouldn’t still be here, you don’t belong.”

✦ ✦ ✦

That kind of whisper does not usually show up in theory. It shows up in real moments. In actual rooms. In decisions you never planned to make under pressure.

I felt it land hard a few years ago in a situation I never saw coming.

GiveSendGo was invited to testify before the Canadian Parliament.

If you remember the Freedom Convoy, you will remember that it was a mess. Truckers had driven to Ottawa to protest vaccine mandates. Other platforms shut down their fundraising, so they came to us. Canada invoked emergency powers, froze accounts, and made decisions that caught a lot of people off guard. When those actions were reviewed later, the Canadian government wanted us to explain ourselves: “Why didn’t you shut the campaign down?”

My brother Jacob and I were the ones asked to answer for it. He is the co-founder and co-CEO of GiveSendGo. We have built this whole thing together, and we have walked through a lot of fires side by side. This was one of the biggest.

My first reaction was immediate and very familiar:

Nope. Absolutely not. What if we say the wrong thing? What if I’m not smart enough to be in that room?

Because of COVID, the hearings were happening over Zoom. The first hour would be PayPal and GoFundMe explaining why they did shut things down. The second hour was us. No lawyers. No team. Just the two of us, sitting at our desks, on a video call, with the Canadian government.

Well. It was supposed to be just him.

Jacob and I had already decided he would do the talking. He is logical and steady, the kind of person who thinks before he speaks and somehow always sounds smart without trying. My plan was to sit quietly in the background, pray for wisdom, and stay out of the way.

That felt safer. That felt like where I belonged.

The meeting started. We watched the first hour. Then they brought us in. They introduced Jacob and started asking questions, and they were not being gentle. They were pressing hard on why we let the campaign keep going. I was praying the whole time, watching Jacob answer clearly and calmly.

And then his screen froze.

His camera locked up. His audio cut out. His face just sat there, frozen, on everyone’s screens while they tried to reconnect him.

A minute went by. Then another.

Finally, someone said, “Well, Ms. Wilson, we assume you can also testify today.”

I think I died a little.

I wanted to throw up. I do not know policy language. I do not know parliamentary process. I had no idea what they were about to ask me, and even less of an idea what I would do if I could not answer.

And then, out of nowhere, something quiet settled in me.

I don’t have to know what I don’t know. If I don’t know something, I can just say I don’t know.

That was it. That one sentence steadied me.

I took a breath. Said a quick prayer. And I started answering.

Not perfectly. Not eloquently. Just honestly. I shared our reasoning. I answered what I could. I referenced Scripture where it fit. And looking back? I did a good job. Not because I suddenly became more qualified.

Because I stopped disqualifying myself.

That moment showed me something I had been doing for years without realizing it. I had a habit of discounting my own legitimacy because I knew my weaknesses. I knew where I lacked training. I knew where I felt outmatched. And I treated all of that like evidence I should not be there.

But that is not how God looks at me when He puts me in a moment.

God does not wait for me to feel confident before He moves. He promises His presence. He promises His wisdom. He promises the words I will need when words are needed. He does not hand me a binder and tell me to prepare for the test—He hands me Himself and tells me to trust Him in the room.

Calling often becomes clearest at the exact moment we realize we aren't enough on our own.

That is almost always how it works.

✦ ✦ ✦

Doubt does not usually stay vague. It needs something to grab onto, so it finds something concrete: The degree you do not have. The certification you never finished. The years of experience you think should be longer by now. The resume that looks a little thinner than the ones sitting next to yours.

We tell ourselves we will feel legitimate once we are more prepared, once we have taken the class, gotten the credential, logged the hours, earned the title.

We assume confidence is the prerequisite for leadership, when really, it’s the byproduct.

I want to be clear: Preparation is not the problem. Learning matters. Growth matters. There are seasons when credentials are helpful and even necessary. I am not anti-effort. I am not telling you to skip the work.

But somewhere along the way, we started believing that readiness has to come before obedience. And the second we believe that, we have quietly shifted the foundation of our leadership away from God and onto ourselves.

Scripture does not back that up. Honestly, Scripture is almost always doing the opposite. God rarely waits for someone to feel ready before He calls them. He calls first. He prepares them in the going.

Readiness is something He builds in us, not something we hand Him as proof we deserve the assignment. This one is personal for me.

I did not go to college. I do not have any formal education to speak of. I am not what most people would call book smart. If you measured me by my pedigree on paper, I would not look qualified for half the rooms I have ended up in.

And I have ended up in some rooms.

Rooms full of people who speak fluently in numbers and acronyms and strategy. Rooms full of letters after names and degrees on walls. Rooms where intelligence is assumed to look and sound a very particular way. I sit down at the table, I get my coffee, I open my notebook, and then someone starts talking and I think, “I’m sorry, what language is this?”

And I think of all the times I have nodded along, pretending I knew exactly what “they” were talking about.

Smiling. Nodding. Making that thoughtful little “mhm” sound like I was tracking every word. Writing something down in my notebook just to look engaged, even though what I actually wrote was “look up what that means later.” Maybe even chiming in with something safe and vague, like “yeah, that’s a great point,” hoping no one would ask me to expand on it.

I know I am not the only one doing this.

The whole time, the old question is humming in the background:

What am I doing here?

We let it convince us that everyone else earned the seat, and somehow we slipped in through a side door no one noticed.

But that is not humility. That is the lie wearing a really good costume.

✦ ✦ ✦

Even when we get the invitation, comparison can quietly talk us out of believing we belong. We look around the room. We notice who speaks with more confidence, who commands attention without trying, who sounds more articulate, more polished, more at ease. And without even meaning to, we start measuring our legitimacy by how closely we match them.

If I really belonged here, I’d sound like her. If I were meant to be here, this would feel easier.

But it does not stop at how we sound. Oh no. For me, it starts way before I even open my mouth.

It starts when I am getting dressed.

I cannot tell you how many times I have stood in my closet, staring at it, having a full-blown internal debate about an outfit. Is this too dressy? Is it not dressy enough? Is this the kind of room where everyone is in heels, or are they going to be in jeans and I will look like I tried too hard? Is this top too young for me? Is it too old? Should I do my hair down or up? Do I need to lose ten pounds before Tuesday?

Then I walk into the room and immediately start scanning. And before anyone has said a single word about business, I have already disqualified myself on appearance alone.

This is what comparison does. It moves the goalposts of legitimacy somewhere that has nothing to do with calling. Suddenly, belonging is not about whether God put me in this room. It is about whether I am pulled together enough, polished enough, stylish enough, young enough, fit enough, enough enough.

What we are actually doing is letting somebody else’s strengths define our calling. We are letting her articulation make us doubt our voice. We are letting her outfit make us doubt our presence. We are letting her highlight reel decide whether our seat at the table is real. But here’s the thing:

God does not duplicate leaders. He designs them.

When I compare myself to the woman across the table, I am quietly resisting the exact thing that makes my leadership matter. The way I think. The way I tell stories. The way I see people. The way God wired me on purpose. Leadership was never meant to be a performance we work to perfect—it was meant to be a calling we steward.

The woman who keeps looking at the woman next to her is not leading. She is auditioning.

And friend, we were not called to audition.

✦ ✦ ✦

Somewhere along the way, we all started taking personality tests.

Enneagram numbers. DISC profiles. Myers-Briggs. Strengths assessments. I have taken them all. I have a whole vocabulary now for how I am wired. And honestly? I love them. They give us language for things we have always sensed about ourselves but never quite knew how to say out loud.

But here is where it gets sneaky:

Somewhere between “this describes me” and “this defines me,” the lie slips in.

I am a 7. I am an I on the DISC. I am an ENFP. So I cannot do the hard conversation. So I am not the strategic one. So I am not built to lead in that kind of room. So someone else, with a better number or a better letter, must be the one God meant for this. And just like that, I have used a personality test to opt out of obedience.

Imagine for a second if the people in the Bible had to take a personality test before God could use them.

Moses walks into the assessment center. Results come back. “Highly anxious. Poor public speaker. Avoidant under pressure. Tendency to argue with authority. Not recommended for high-stakes leadership.”

Sorry Moses. You are clearly not the guy. We are going to need someone with better communication scores to go talk to Pharaoh.

Noah sits down for his interview. “Isolated. Obsessive. Builds things nobody asked for. Talks to himself. Difficulty reading the room. Possibly delusional.”

Yeah, Noah, we are going to pass. The whole ark thing is honestly a red flag.

Abraham gets his results back at age seventy-five. “Subject is elderly. No clear plan. Willing to leave everything based on a feeling. Highly impractical. Does not respond well to logical timelines.”

Abraham, buddy. We are going to need you to retire. Whoever told you to start a nation at this age clearly did not consult your file.

Gideon? Hiding in a winepress. Esther? Terrified. Peter? Impulsive to the point of cutting off a guy’s ear. Jonah? Literally ran in the opposite direction of his assignment. None of these people would have passed the test.

And that is the whole point.

God has never once needed a personality profile to call someone. He calls the bold and the quiet. The strategic and the relational. The eloquent and the tongue-tied. The reluctant and the ready. Not one person in Scripture got out of the assignment because of how they were wired.

A personality test can tell you how you tend to respond. It cannot tell you what God is going to ask of you. It can describe your defaults. It cannot define the limits of your obedience. When description turns into excuse, preference becomes calling, and comfort gets called conviction, we are no longer being honest about how we are wired. We are using how we are wired as a way out.

Your personality shapes how you lead. It does not decide whether you are called to.

✦ ✦ ✦

For some women, the lie of legitimacy is not about ability at all. It is about timing. Too young to be taken seriously yet. Too old to start something new now. Too late to be considered for the thing you would have been perfect for ten years ago. Too early in your career to be taken seriously in this one.

And honestly, we have been measuring ourselves against a timeline our whole lives.

There is an invisible schedule most women feel from a very young age. Graduate by this age. Get married by this age. Have kids by this age. Buy a house by this age. Be established in your career by this age. Get the promotion by this age. Be debt free, in shape, well-traveled, mortgage-paid, retirement-funded, and emotionally healed by this age.

Nobody handed us this list, but somehow we all got it.

And the second one of those boxes does not get checked on time, the lie shows up.

Everyone else is ahead of me. I must have done something wrong.

When leadership does not unfold the way we pictured it, we quietly start reading delay as disqualification. The longer the waiting goes on, the more sure we get that we missed something. That we made the wrong call somewhere. That the train left and we were not on it.

A lot of women step out of visible leadership for seasons that matter deeply: raising kids, caring for a parent, walking through a hard chapter, supporting a spouse through a transition. Real work. Sacred work. And almost entirely unseen work.

Then one day they look up, ready to step back in, and the questions come: Am I too late? Have I fallen behind? Has the world moved on without me?

I have been that woman.

I remember being in my thirties, sitting in the car with Dan, driving around our town. We were renting. First, an apartment. Then a townhouse. Then a farmhouse. They were all great homes for the season we were in. But we had never owned one.

One afternoon we drove past a house with a For Sale sign in the yard, and Dan said, “We are never going to own a house. All our friends have bought houses. We are not even close.”

I remember sitting in that car feeling so behind.

All around us, friends were buying houses, settling in, and hitting the milestones we thought we were supposed to have hit by then. We were in ministry—we were not making the kind of money that buys you a house. And it felt like everyone else had been handed the playbook and we had been handed a different one entirely.

I look back at that conversation now and almost want to reach through time and squeeze that woman’s hand. Because we live in a more beautiful home than I could ever have imagined, one that fits our family, fits our life, and fits the season God eventually brought us into.

His timing was not rushed, and it was not early. It was exactly right. What felt like “falling behind” was actually “being formed.”

Here is something I think we forget when we read the Bible: We read these stories like they happened over a weekend.

Take Joseph. We open Genesis and read about his dreams, his brothers, the coat, the betrayal. A chapter or two later, he is in Egypt. In another chapter, he is in prison. A few more, and he is second in command of an entire nation. We close the Bible feeling inspired and think, “Wow, look how God worked it all out.”

But we did not feel the wait.

We did not sit in the years he spent as a slave in a foreign household with no idea why God let him get sold by his own family. We did not sit in the years he spent in a prison cell for something he did not do, after he had finally done the right thing. We did not sit in the years he spent storing up grain, doing the daily, boring, unglamorous work of administration before any of his brothers ever showed up looking for food.

Joseph’s story takes us about thirty minutes to read. It took him decades to live.

And he is not the only one.

Moses spent forty years tending sheep on the back side of a desert before God ever spoke from a burning bush. Forty years where nothing in his life would have looked like leadership development. David was anointed king as a teenager and did not actually sit on the throne until he was around thirty. He spent the years in between hiding in caves while the current king tried to kill him.

None of them were on a fast track. None of them were on the schedule any of us would have written. And every single one of them spent the longest stretch of their story in the part we read in a single sitting.

The waiting is the work.

The years that feel like nothing is happening are very rarely years where nothing is happening. They are the years where God is doing the slow, hidden, quiet, formation work that no one will ever see and that will eventually be the reason you can carry what He is preparing you for.

You are not late. You are not behind. You are not off-schedule. There is no schedule. There is only calling, and calling has its own timing.

The years you thought disqualified you may be the years that prepared you most.

A while back, Jacob and I got asked to be plenary speakers at C12’s Current conference. If you are not familiar, C12 is a Christian CEO and business owner network. This was not a small room. There were thousands of CEOs and successful business owners there. People who had built companies, scaled organizations, led teams. Carried responsibilities heavier than most people will ever carry. It was an honor. A real one.

Here is what surprised me: As I headed to the conference, I was not nervous about speaking.

I have spoken a lot over the years. I have gotten more comfortable telling stories. Standing on a stage does not rattle me the way it used to. But the day before we were scheduled to speak, we were just sitting in sessions, soaking in the environment. At lunchtime I found a seat at a table that filled up fast. We talked. We swapped stories. It was actually really nice. Comfortable, even. And then, for just a second, the conversation paused. I sat there quietly and looked around the room.

And my stomach sank.

It was not panic. It was something slower than that. A slow, sinking awareness of where I was and who I was sitting with. I started noticing the suits. The easy confidence. The way everyone seemed to speak in this fluent business shorthand at every table around me. These were people who carried themselves with a kind of polish I have never quite figured out how to wear. They had more education than me, more formal training. They had resumes I could not even compete with on paper. Suddenly the weight of what we were about to do landed on me, we were going to stand on a stage. And speak. To them.

This is not a crowd I am qualified to speak to.

They are smarter than me. More educated. More articulate. They know numbers and strategy and systems in ways that I genuinely do not. They have credentials I have never earned and probably never will. Now understand, I know what I bring to the table. I do not minimize it. I know the stories of God’s faithfulness Jacob and I carry. I know the testimony of what God has built and sustained through GiveSendGo. I am not sitting here pretending I have nothing to say.

But in that moment, the question was not whether God had been faithful.

The question was whether I was credible enough for this room. And that, right there, is exactly how this lie works: It does not flat-out deny the calling. That would be too obvious. Instead it reframes the room—it tells you the audience is too advanced. The setting is too impressive. The moment is somehow above your pay grade. It convinces you that your story might be meaningful somewhere, just not here.

I did not walk away from that table. I did not cancel the talk. But I had to sit with the question quietly humming under my ribs.

Do I really belong on that stage?

Then something settled in me. If my legitimacy is decided by pedigree, then my calling will always feel fragile. But if my legitimacy comes from God, then obedience is enough. That realization did not make me suddenly feel smarter. It did not erase what I know I lack. But it anchored me back into the truth: God did not ask me to be the most educated person in the room. He asked me to be faithful with what He has put in my hands.

And that my friend, that is the only assignment any of us were ever actually given.

Around this same season, I started reading through the New Testament again, this time with a study guide and the Bema podcast playing in the background. Dan had given me a journaling Bible, the kind designed for notes and margins and the slow kind of reading where you actually stop and think.

Usually when reading through the Bible I start in the Old Testament. I do not know why— probably because it is at the beginning of the book— but this time I decided to start with Matthew. I have read Matthew so many times. And like most people, I usually skim the genealogy. You know it is there. You recognize a few of the names. You understand why it is included. And then you move on to the part where the actual story kicks in.

But this time I slowed down. The study guide opened with a simple question: Who is the author, and who is he writing to? Matthew was writing to Jewish readers, and that mattered more than I realized. Because for Jewish people, a genealogy was not filler, it was pedigree. It told you who someone was— where they came from, who their people were, who they had a right to be associated with. It established legitimacy. It traced lineage. It declared who belonged. And here is the thing: Matthew’s audience would have loved a genealogy. They would have leaned in. They would have read it like a resume. They were waiting to see how impressive the family tree of this Messiah was going to be.

So Matthew sits down to write, and he starts naming names. Almost immediately, something weird happens- he names women. That alone is unusual. Genealogies named men. Women got mentioned only when there was a very specific reason. And the women Matthew picks are not the women you would pick if you were trying to impress anyone.

Let me walk you through it, because when I realized this it stopped me in the middle of my typical genealogy skimming. Matthew gets a few names in, and he says Judah was the father of Perez and Zerah by Tamar.

If you know her story, you know it is complicated. And messy. And uncomfortable. Tamar was married to two brothers who both died. She was left with no protection, no provision, no future. Eventually, in desperation, she disguised herself as a prostitute, slept with her father-in-law Judah, and got pregnant.

This is not a story you highlight if you are trying to build credibility. This is the kind of story you bury at the bottom of the family tree and hope nobody asks about at Thanksgiving. But Matthew names her. Right there. On purpose.

A few names later, he writes that Salmon was the father of Boaz by Rahab. Rahab. The prostitute. From Jericho. The Gentile woman who hid the Israelite spies. She was not a footnote. Not tucked away. Named right out in the open in the bloodline of the Messiah.

Then he names Ruth, the mother of Obed. Ruth was not even Jewish, she was a Moabite. An outsider. The Moabites were the kind of people the Israelites were told not to mix with. By lineage alone, Ruth had no business being in this story.

And then he gets to David. But Matthew does not write what you would expect. He does not say Solomon was born to David and Bathsheba. He says Solomon was born to David by the wife of Uriah. If you know the story, you know exactly why that phrasing stings.

David took another man’s wife. He slept with her, and got her pregnant. And then he had her husband, Uriah, killed to cover it up. Matthew does not soften that. He does not sanitize it. He does not skip it. He names it. If genealogies are about pedigree, this is not how you write one. Matthew knew exactly what he was doing.

He was a Jew writing to Jews who were obsessed with pedigree. And right out of the gate, in the very first paragraph of his Gospel, he is dismantling it. He is telling them, “You think you know what qualifies someone to be part of God’s story. Let me show you who God actually used.”

Here is what really stopped me- when I remembered who Matthew himself was, the whole thing hit even deeper. Matthew was a tax collector—a Jew who worked for the Roman government collecting money from his own people. He was seen as a sellout. A traitor. Someone who had compromised his faith and his heritage for a paycheck. He was not respected, he was not admired. He was not the kind of guy anyone was inviting to lead a Bible study.

And he is the one God chose to write this Gospel.

The man whose entire reputation said “you do not belong” is the man God entrusted with telling the world about Jesus. Pedigree has never been the point. God has always, always worked through unlikely people. Outsiders, people with complicated stories. People whose pasts do not look impressive on a resume. People who would have been told by every cultural standard around them that they were not qualified. This is the God of second chances, the God of grace. The God whose redemption does not check your transcript at the door.

The genealogy of Jesus is not a list of impressive credentials. It is a quiet, deliberate declaration that God’s calling has always been bigger than human standards of legitimacy.

And if it was bigger then, it is still bigger now.

✦ ✦ ✦

The Truth That Replaces the Lie

The lie says, “I am not qualified enough to lead here.”

And it sounds reasonable. It sounds responsible. It even sounds a little humble. But here is what that lie does over time, it turns into paralysis. It keeps us watching from the edges of rooms we were invited into. It keeps us hesitating on assignments God already gave us. It teaches us to delay obedience until confidence finally shows up.

The truth tells a completely different story.

Calling does not need my confidence to be valid. It needs my obedience. God has never once depended on human certainty to accomplish His purposes. He moves through willingness. He moves through faithfulness. He moves through people who are willing to take the next step before they have all the answers.

Legitimacy, in God’s economy, is not something I earn through my own preparation. It is not something I prove through my own performance. It is something I receive through trust. It is established by God, not confirmed by people. And it grows as I walk, not as I wait. Leadership shaped by obedience does not rush to prove itself. It does not panic when recognition is delayed. It does not need the room to validate it. It is anchored in trust, not approval. It is free from the exhausting, never-ending pressure to keep earning the right to be there. Because belonging has already been given. The invitation was never to feel qualified. The invitation has always been to trust the One who called you.

You do not have to keep proving you belong in the room. You do not have to keep apologizing for the seat you were given. You do not have to wait until you feel ready to walk in obedience. You can simply step forward, exactly as you are, with all your questions still hanging in the air, and trust that the One who called you knows what He is doing.

That is freedom. That is rest. That is what leadership was always meant to feel like.

I belong where God has called me, even when I feel unqualified.

That is the truth that replaces the lie.

Chapter Seven

The Lie of Criticism

Lie: If people are upset, I must be doing something wrong.

You know the feeling.

Someone said something to you earlier today and you cannot stop thinking about it. It is not even something huge. A comment in a meeting. A weird tone in a text. A look from someone at the school pickup line. A complaint that landed on your desk that was probably about something else but somehow still felt personal.

You are doing dishes and replaying it. You are driving home and replaying it. You are lying in bed at eleven o'clock and replaying it.

You are drafting a response in your head that nobody will ever hear. You are crafting the perfect, calm, dignified reply. You are wondering if you should send the email. You are wondering if you should say something. You are wondering if you are overreacting.

That is what this chapter is about.

Because somewhere along the way, a lot of us got the idea that if someone is unhappy with us, it means we did something wrong. And we cannot rest until we figure out what.

✦ ✦ ✦

I did not grow up feeling criticized. I was not raised in an environment where people constantly pointed out what I was doing wrong, and I did not carry childhood wounds from harsh correction or relentless disapproval.

What shaped me instead was something quieter, and honestly harder to see at first: I was a people pleaser.

I liked peace and harmony, and I wanted people to be happy and getting along. I wanted them to enjoy being around me. Getting in trouble made me uncomfortable, and confrontation was something I avoided whenever I could. What I really wanted was friends and a sense that things felt light and good and smooth.

I liked being fun. I liked being liked.

So early on, I learned how to avoid friction. If there was tension, I smoothed it. If conflict showed up, I delayed it or rushed through it just to get it over with. Not because I was dishonest or because I lacked conviction, but because peace felt safe and kind and right. For a long time, that approach worked… until leadership required something different.

✦ ✦ ✦

When we started GiveSendGo, it felt like the perfect intersection of conviction and comfort. It was a platform centered around generosity and where the hope of Jesus was something people celebrated. People liked it and affirmed it. Churches embraced it, families shared it, and everyday believers finally had a place to fundraise that aligned with their faith.

It felt good and peaceful, like leadership without friction.

And then, one morning I will never forget, about five years in, that peace disappeared overnight. I woke up to a phone full of messages. Voicemails, texts, missed calls. I opened GiveSendGo and saw something I had never seen before- THOUSANDS of emails. At that point in our growth, that was unheard of.

And most of them were angry.

Threatening, vicious, hateful. People were leaving horrific voicemails, flooding our social media, saying awful things about me, my family, and Jacob. They found my children on social media and messaged them, telling them they should kill themselves for having me as their mother. I had no idea what was happening. I just knew something had exploded.

As we started digging, we realized what had triggered it: a campaign had been created on GiveSendGo for a young man named Kyle Rittenhouse. He had been deplatformed from other fundraising platforms and was now trying to raise money for his legal defense, and in my people-pleasing instinct, my very first thought was immediate and reactive.

How do we stop the hate? How do we make people happy?

It became clear very quickly that there was no decision that would satisfy everyone. Every possible option came with backlash, and every path forward meant someone would be furious.

Christians were writing to us saying, “How dare you allow this campaign.” Non-Christians were writing the same thing. Our website was under attack, with DDoS attacks ( this stands for Distributed Denial-of-Service), which is a malicious cyber attack that floods a website, server, or network with overwhelming internet traffic to make it crash or stop working for real users. The pressure was relentless, the accusations were personal, and the fear felt very real.

People were calling my church and telling the secretary that a white supremacist terrorist attended there. They reached out to past employers. They contacted Dan and told him he should fire me. I remember laughing at that one for a moment, because I genuinely did not enjoy it when I had to balance working in his IT company and would have been just fine being let go. But humor only carried me so far.

This was not a disagreement. This was public condemnation. And it forced a question I had never had to answer before:

What do we do when doing the right thing doesn't bring a feeling of peace?

✦ ✦ ✦

Jacob and I knew we could not make a knee-jerk decision. Everything in us wanted relief, silence, calm, an end to the noise. But reacting out of fear would have only made things worse. So we stopped.

We prayed and we fasted, and we asked God for clarity rather than comfort. We reached out to people we trusted, people with faith and discernment. I called my pastor, Larry. I called a Christian businessman in our community that I respected deeply, Morgan. We did not ask them what would be popular. We asked them what would be right. More than anything, I didn't want to drag God's name through the mud.

The conclusion was not simple, but it was clear: this was not about endorsing a person. It was about standing behind a principle. One of the foundational freedoms in the United States is the presumption of innocence and the right to a fair trial by jury. Kyle Rittenhouse was raising money for his legal defense. We did not know him, and we did not know the full story. And that was exactly the point.

We cannot allow social media mobs to decide guilt or innocence based on three-second clips. The justice system is not perfect, but it matters. Allowing that campaign was not about agreement. It was about principle.

So we allowed it.

I wish I could tell you that once Kyle Rittenhouse was found not guilty, everything settled down.

It didn't.

If anything, our reputation became even more defined. GiveSendGo was now known as the platform that wasn't going to cancel people simply because public opinion demanded it. The criticism kept coming. National media. Social media. Angry emails. Accusations. I found myself carrying the weight of everyone's disapproval, and I was wrestling it all out with God.

Deep down, I didn't want the world to hate me.

One afternoon, while I was sitting at my desk, Kenzie walked in looking upset.

"Mom...Dwight called me stupid."

I looked up and asked,

"Well... are you stupid?"

She looked completely offended.

"No!"

"Okay," I said. “Then who cares? What if he walked in here and called you a banana?"

She looked at me like I had lost my mind.

"A banana?"

"Yeah. Would you get upset?"

She started smiling. "No."

"Would you argue with him?"

"No."

"Would you explain that you aren't yellow and don't have a peel?"

By now she was laughing.

I said, "Kenzie, if someone calls you something that isn't true, just pretend they're calling you a banana. Laugh it off. They look sillier than you do."

As I heard myself saying those words to her, it was like the Lord quietly turned them back toward me:

"Exactly, Heather. When people call you things that aren't true, pretend they're just calling you a banana."

That simple conversation stayed with me. In the middle of one of the hardest seasons of criticism I had ever faced, God used my own words to my daughter to teach me one of the greatest leadership lessons of my life.

It stayed with me so deeply that I eventually wrote a children's book called I Am Not a Banana.

The criticism didn't disappear. The headlines didn't stop. People didn't suddenly understand us. But every time another accusation came, I found myself smiling and thinking, I'm not a banana.

God was teaching me that criticism doesn't automatically become truth simply because it's loud. This taught me something I could not have learned any other way. The weight of standing firm does not belong to one kind of story. It shows up anytime you make a decision you believe is right and others disagree. It is the moment you choose a direction for your company and feel the temperature change in the room. It looks like the meeting where people stop nodding, the email that lands with pushback instead of affirmation, the conversation where someone questions your judgment, your motives, or your heart.

The temptation to soften or reverse the choice just to restore ease and approval. Standing firm means carrying disagreement without immediately trying to resolve it. It means allowing people to be disappointed without scrambling to earn back their approval, and trusting discernment even when affirmation is absent.

This is where leadership becomes costly. Not because the decision was wrong, but because disagreement exposes our desire to be liked and understood.

✦ ✦ ✦

Most women will never wake up to thousands of emails or national attention. Most criticism does not arrive as a public explosion. It shows up in our daily life, personally, and far more often than we want to admit.

It looks like your boss raising their voice about something that was your fault, or something that was not. Either way, your body reacts before your wisdom does. You want to defend yourself or explain or push back or just shut down.

It looks like an email filled with half-truths or outright lies, where every sentence feels unfair and you start drafting a response in your head before you have even finished reading it.

It looks like a fight with your husband where something sharp is said and everything in you wants the last word. Right now. Not after prayer. Now.

Criticism pressures you to react instead of pausing to discern, to pray, or to slow down. It just wants a response. But knee-jerk reactions, whether in big moments or small ones, almost always come from fear. Fear of being misunderstood, fear of losing control, fear of being seen as wrong.

And the wisdom is the same no matter the size of the moment. Stop. Slow down. Create space before you respond. Not because silence fixes everything, but because clarity rarely shows up in urgency. God does not rush us into reactions. He invites us into discernment.

Sometimes the most faithful response to criticism is not a better argument, but a pause.

✦ ✦ ✦

Social media was a huge part of this for me.

When the criticism started, my instinct was to respond to everyone. Every comment and every accusation and every lie. I wanted to clarify and defend and correct the record. And I told myself I was doing it for the right reasons. That I was protecting the truth and guarding the company. So I engaged. I responded and I explained, jumping into comment threads and drafting careful statements as fast as I could write them. And almost every time, it made things worse.

Not just for me emotionally, but for the company I was responsible for leading. The more I talked, the longer the criticism lasted. The more I explained, the more people twisted my words. The more I tried to control the narrative, the more reactive and exhausted I became. What I did not understand at first was that social media does not reward discernment. It rewards engagement, speed, and outrage. And once you step into that current, it is very hard to step back out.

There is a kind of false responsibility that creeps in during moments like that. A belief that if something untrue is said publicly, you are obligated to publicly correct it. That if people misunderstand you, you owe them an explanation. That if your name is attached to something, you must defend it everywhere it appears. But leadership does not require constant clarification, and silence is not the same thing as surrender.

For me, discernment eventually meant learning to stop talking. It meant stepping back, letting things pass without my commentary attached to them, and trusting that truth does not need my constant defense in order to survive.

That was not easy. Everything in me wanted to keep engaging, to keep explaining, to jump back in and fix it. But over time, I began to see that my words were not bringing resolution. They were extending the conflict. And my presence in those spaces was not helping the people I was leading. It was draining me and distracting me from the work God had actually entrusted to me.

Stepping away from the noise became one of the most important leadership lessons I learned in this season.

One of the most grounding truths I have learned is this: This too shall pass. No matter how big it feels in the moment. No matter how unfair it seems. No matter how loud it gets. The news cycle proves it over and over again. What feels all-consuming today will be replaced by something else tomorrow. When you stand firm, take the criticism, and keep moving forward, time does a lot of the work for you.

✦ ✦ ✦

Discernment Versus Defensiveness

Criticism does not always show up because you did something wrong. Sometimes it shows up because you stood still when others wanted you to move. Because you refused to bend. Because you chose obedience over appeasement.

That season taught me the difference between discernment and defensiveness. Defensiveness wants the pain to stop. Discernment wants to honor God. Defensiveness reacts quickly. Discernment slows down. This matters, because not all criticism should be treated the same way. Some criticism is a gift. Some is a distraction. And some is simply noise.

There are times when criticism is pointing out something real, such as when we missed something or handled a situation poorly. When pride crept in or fear drove a decision. Wisdom listens in those moments. Discernment invites a small, trusted group of people into the conversation and asks honest questions: Is there truth here? Is there something I need to own? Is God trying to correct me?

Healthy leaders do not ignore correction. They invite it, but only from the right voices.

Then there are moments when criticism comes not because you are wrong, but because you are right. Because your obedience disrupts someone else’s comfort. That is where defensiveness becomes tempting. Defensiveness feels like strength, but it is usually fear in disguise. It shows up as over-explaining, as needing the last word, as crafting the perfect response that will finally make people understand. It rarely works.

Jesus did not respond to every accusation or chase down every false story or correct every misunderstanding. He spoke when truth needed to be spoken, and He remained silent when explanation would not bring understanding. That restraint was not weakness—it was leadership.

One of the clearest pictures of this in Scripture is Peter walking on water. Peter steps out of the boat because Jesus calls him. He does not move impulsively. He steps out because Jesus invites him to. And as long as Peter keeps his eyes on Jesus, he does the impossible: he walks on water. Then the wind picks up, the waves get louder, and the environment starts demanding his attention. The moment Peter shifts his focus away from Jesus and onto what is happening around him, fear takes over. He starts to sink. Nothing about Jesus changed in that moment. The calling did not change. The invitation did not change. What changed was where Peter was looking.

Criticism works the same way. As long as your attention stays fixed on Jesus, His calling, His voice, His truth, you can keep moving forward even when what you are doing feels impossible, and when the criticism is loud. But the moment your focus shifts to the noise, the reactions, the opinions swirling around you, fear creeps in. And suddenly, you are no longer discerning. You are reacting.

You stop asking, “What did God ask me to do?” and start asking, “How do I make this stop?”

This is why knowing whose voice to trust is an imperative part of leadership. You need people who can speak truth when you are wrong. People who love you enough to say hard things and help you course-correct when pride, fear, or insecurity shows up. And you also need people who will stand with you when you are being criticized for doing the right thing. People who remind you of what God asked you to do when the noise gets loud.

People who help you grow a backbone without growing a hard heart.

✦ ✦ ✦

I wish I could say this lesson only applied in moments of public criticism or high-stakes leadership. But I have learned it just as clearly in quiet, ordinary moments where nothing needed to be said, but everything in me wanted to say something anyway.

I had taken Kenzie who was 11 years old, to the pediatrician for her annual checkup. I knew she was going to need a couple of shots, and she had been nervous about it all day. By the time we got to the office, she was holding it together, but I could see the tears right at the edge. When it was time for the shots, it all came out. She started to panic. She was flailing and crying, and I was trying everything I could think of. I pulled out my phone and started playing silly videos, hoping she would laugh or at least get distracted. She was not having it.

At some point, my patience snapped just a little. I remember saying, “Kenzie, you need to knock it off. I will hold you down if I have to. This is not a big deal. It’s a quick pinch. You are going to be fine.” She was still upset, and in the middle of all of it, the nurse practitioner giving the shots made a comment.

“When I was little,” she said, “I was more afraid of my parents than I was of the shot.”

It was a quick comment, probably offhand and probably not even meant to cut. But it landed hard. I did not say anything in the moment. I was focused on Kenzie, trying to get her through it. She got the shots, realized they did not hurt, and almost immediately started giggling, like nothing had happened.

We walked out, and on the surface, the moment was over.

But it was not over in my head.

That one comment replayed over and over. “You’re not doing a good job. You don’t have control. She should be more afraid of you.”

I thought about what I should have said, what I could have said, how I could have put her in her place. The amount of space I let that moment take up in my mind was completely out of proportion to what actually happened. It was a flippant comment from someone I would probably never see again. It did not require a response, and it did not need to be addressed. And yet, I kept picking it back up.

That is what criticism often does. It does not always demand a public response. Sometimes it just quietly takes up residence in our thoughts, where we replay it and rehearse imaginary conversations and craft the perfect comeback that no one will ever hear. We give it far more authority than it deserves.

Scripture tells us to take every thought captive and make it obedient to Christ. That instruction matters here, because not every thought that enters your mind deserves to stay. Not every criticism deserves your attention, and not every moment needs to be carried forward. That comment did not define my parenting. It did not require correction, and it did not deserve the real estate I gave it.

Not all criticism asks for a response. But every criticism asks whether you will give it authority.

Leadership, motherhood, ministry, life… all of it will bring moments like this. Not loud criticism or public outrage, but small, cutting comments that tempt us to defend, explain, or replay. Discernment teaches us that we do not have to pick up every criticism handed to us. Some things are meant to fall to the ground and stay there.

✦ ✦ ✦

Once you realize that not every criticism deserves a response, the next question becomes unavoidable. Whose voice actually gets to shape me? When criticism is loud, it all starts to sound important. Every opinion feels urgent, and every reaction feels like it requires a response. If you are not careful, you begin treating every voice as authoritative just because it showed up.

I did that. Especially early on, I confused exposure with authority. If someone could see the decision, comment on the decision, or criticize the decision, I assumed they somehow had a say in it. Social media only amplified that. Everyone had access, and I treated access like assignment.

But access does not equal authority.

Just because someone can speak into your situation does not mean they were meant to shape it. I had to learn to slow down and ask better questions. Do they actually know me? Do they understand the responsibility I am carrying? Will they still be here when the consequences of this decision show up? Over time, I realized I needed clear categories for the voices I allowed into my leadership. Not to shut people out, but to stay grounded.

There are voices God assigns. Scripture, prayer, the steady conviction of the Holy Spirit: These are non-negotiable. They anchor everything else. There are also trusted human voices- A small circle of people who know me, know the work, and understand the cost of the decisions I am making. People who will tell me the truth when I am wrong, and who will stand with me when I am being criticized for doing the right thing. Those voices earn their weight.

Then there are the others. The comments, the emails, the opinions that arrive after the fact with no responsibility for the outcome. These voices are not evil, they are just not authoritative. And leadership requires the courage to let them exist without letting them lead.

Jesus modeled this perfectly. He listened closely to the Father, invited a few trusted voices into His inner circle, and let the crowd misunderstand Him.

Choosing whose voice matters is not arrogance. It is obedience.

Leadership becomes impossible when every opinion gets a vote. But it becomes steady when you decide ahead of time whose voices will shape you, and whose voices you will simply acknowledge and release.

✦ ✦ ✦

The Truth That Replaces the Lie

The lie says, if people are upset, I must be doing something wrong. It sounds reasonable, even responsible. But it is not true. Faithfulness is measured by obedience, not approval. Peace is not the absence of criticism. Peace is alignment with God. The kind of peace that comes from knowing you listened, discerned, prayed, and moved forward with integrity, even when the outcome was uncomfortable.

That season changed me, not because the criticism was louder than anything I had faced before, but because it revealed how deeply I had equated peace with rightness. I had assumed that if things felt calm, I must be aligned. And if things felt tense, I must have missed something.

God gently, firmly showed me otherwise. He showed me that obedience would sometimes disrupt the very harmony I loved. That doing the right thing could make people uncomfortable. And that standing for principle might cost approval. None of that meant I was wrong.

This truth does not make criticism easy. It makes it clearer. It gives you a different set of questions to ask when criticism shows up. Not “How do I make this stop?” but “Did I listen to God?” Not “How do I get everyone back on my side?” but “Did I move with integrity?”

Faithfulness does not require you to be perfect. It requires you to be honest and teachable. Willing to examine criticism without being ruled by it. Sometimes criticism is an invitation to grow. Other times, it is simply the cost of leading well. Discernment helps you tell the difference. When you know whose voice you are listening to, you can stand steady even when others are upset. You can pause instead of react, and you can choose obedience over appeasement without becoming defensive or hardened.

This truth frees you from the exhausting work of trying to manage everyone else's reactions. It releases you from the need to constantly explain, correct, or defend yourself. It anchors you in something far steadier than approval. You may still feel the discomfort, and you may still wish the criticism would fade. But you no longer confuse it with wrongdoing.

Criticism will come. Not because you are reckless, but because leadership requires courage. Because standing for principle will always upset someone. Because faithfulness is rarely comfortable.

Some criticism is an invitation to grow.

Some criticism is simply someone calling you a banana.

Wisdom is learning the difference.

God's approval is steadier than public opinion. His calling is clearer than criticism. And obedience, even when costly, is never wasted.

Discomfort does not mean disobedience.

And that is the truth that replaces the lie.

BUILT TO SHINE

For the Woman Leading with Faith in the Business World

Chapter Eight

The Lie of Compartmentalization

Lie: I have to hide my faith to be successful in business.

There was a season when I worked at a restaurant as a waitress. It was not a ministry environment. It was fast-paced, noisy, and transactional. People knew I went to church, the same way people know what car you drive or where you live. It was information, not formation.

A new girl started working there, and we became friends. Between shifts and side work, we would talk when we could. Over time, she began opening up to me about her marriage. She was struggling. Her husband was struggling. She was searching for answers, for hope, for something to hold onto.

I listened, shared advice, and became friends with her. And quietly, persistently, I felt the Lord prompting me to do more than just offer good marriage counsel. I felt Him nudging me to tell her about Jesus, to invite her to church. To give her more than practical advice and actually offer her the hope that had shaped my own life and marriage. But I reasoned myself out of it.

I told myself I did not want to be weird. I did not want to cross a line. I did not want her to feel uncomfortable or think I was pushing something on her. I did not want to lose her trust or have her stop confiding in me. That I would do it later.

And beneath all of that, I told myself a familiar lie: She already knows I’m a Christian. That’s enough.

We say this often. We assume proximity equals witness, and that being associated with church attendance is the same as sharing good news. But if we truly believe the gospel is good news, then treating it as something too awkward to offer is its own kind of contradiction. There was a saying that showed up on t-shirts a few years back “Share the Gospel and if needed, use words.” As Christ followers, sharing the Gospel will always use words.

I didn’t end up sharing Jesus with her directly… not because I did not believe it, but because I compartmentalized my faith. I reasoned that there would be a more appropriate time, so I chose to be palatable instead of present.

Eventually, she stopped working at the restaurant. Months later, she came back in with her husband and sat in my section. When I walked up to the table, she lit up. We hugged. We caught up. And then she said something that caused my stomach to drop. She told me that a friend had invited them to church, and that they had started attending together. Just the week before, they had both been baptized. I was thrilled for them. Truly. God had done what He always does. He had pursued them, restored them, and met them where they were.

Beneath the joy was a sharp, quiet personal grief.

I realized I had missed something: God had wanted to use me. He had invited me into their story, not because He needed me, but because He wanted me to participate—and I had opted out. Not out of rebellion, but out of fear and concern for perception and a desire to remain professionally and socially safe. God still accomplished His purpose…He always does. But He had to choose someone else.

That moment changed me. Not with shame, but with resolve. I never wanted to miss out on an adventure God had for me because I was more concerned with what people might think of me than with who God was inviting me to be.

Compartmentalization does not just hide faith from others. It robs us of obedience. It costs us participation.

✦ ✦ ✦

There is this strange tension in leadership sometimes where it can feel like faith and professionalism are somehow competing with each other.

Professionalism is often defined as neutrality. Emotional restraint. Carefully curated language. Faith, on the other hand, is often treated like something personal that should stay private because people are worried it will make things uncomfortable or divisive. And after a while it can start feeling like you have to choose between being respected professionally or being open about your faith.

I actually think faith should make us stronger leaders, not weaker ones. Faith, at its core, shapes virtues that leadership desperately needs: integrity, stewardship, accountability, courage, humility. A long-term view of impact rather than short-term wins.

The more leadership books I read, the more I started noticing how many leadership principles trace back to biblical principles, whether people acknowledge it or not. Servant leadership. Integrity. Humility. Discipline. Building trust. Caring about people well. Thinking long term instead of chasing quick success. So much of what the business world praises as great leadership is really just Godly wisdom showing up in different language.

That is what I find interesting sometimes, leadership culture will celebrate those principles all day long, but then faith itself can still feel like something you are supposed to keep separate from leadership. It’s like people are comfortable with the principles as long as you do not talk too much about where they came from. The real issue is not that faith undermines professionalism—it is that faith resists control. Faith introduces moral clarity into environments that often thrive on flexibility. It raises questions that cannot always be solved by spreadsheets or consensus, and it reminds us that leadership is not just about outcomes but about obedience, responsibility, and truth.

So in order to maintain what we feel is control, faith is reduced to something private. Inspirational at best. Irrelevant at worst.

But every leader is guided by something. Every decision flows from a worldview. The only difference is whether that worldview is acknowledged or hidden. Compartmentalization allows us to pretend our leadership is objective. Faith-informed leadership requires us to admit that values shape choices, priorities, and sacrifices. This does not mean preaching in meetings or inserting Scripture into every conversation. It means refusing to pretend that faith stops at the office door, and allowing belief to inform how we treat people, how we measure success, and how we respond to pressure.

Professionalism is not compromised by faith. It is clarified by it.

✦ ✦ ✦

Compartmentalization becomes most tempting when faith begins to cost something tangible: Revenue goals. Investor expectations. Donor relationships. Partnerships. Contracts. Growth opportunities. These moments rarely present themselves as moral crises; they arrive disguised as strategic decisions, reasonable compromises, and necessary adjustments.

We tell ourselves this is just how business works. We separate survival from stewardship, justify decisions by postponing principles, and convince ourselves that we can do good later, once things are more stable and we have more influence.

But faith does not function on delay.

When money is on the line, compartmentalization offers a seductive solution, keep faith internal. Make the decision that secures the outcome, then revisit conviction later. One exception becomes a habit. One compromise becomes a precedent. One moment of silence becomes a culture.

Leaders who compartmentalize their faith often feel the tension long before they can articulate it. Something feels off. Decisions may be successful externally but unsettled internally. Wins feel hollow and growth feels heavier than it should. That is the cost of divided leadership. And over time, that division takes a toll that goes deeper than strategy. It becomes emotional and spiritual weariness. Many women describe this as burnout, but often it is not burnout, it is fragmentation. Burnout comes from doing too much. Fragmentation comes from being divided. One needs rest. The other needs integrity.

God did not design us to live compartmentalized lives. He did not design faith to function as a private accessory while leadership operates independently. He formed us as integrated beings: mind, spirit, conviction, and calling woven together. When we attempt to lead in pieces, something inside us strains against it.

Wholeness is not a luxury. It is a requirement for sustainable leadership.

✦ ✦ ✦

Once you truly believe that what you have is good news, separation starts to feel artificial.

From the outside, there are a hundred reasons to say faith does not belong here. It would be easy to keep things clean, quiet, and purely functional. We did not wake up one day and decide to make our platform faith-centered—we decided from the beginning to be honest about who we are.

We started a Hope Team that calls campaign owners and offers to pray with them. We added prayer buttons to the campaigns so people could not only give, but pray. We created a prayer wall. We began including Scripture in our emails, not as marketing or pressure, but as presence.

You raise the standard. You remove the option to cut corners quietly. If you are going to pray with people, you cannot fudge numbers. If you are going to claim integrity, you must practice it relentlessly. If you are going to say faith shapes your leadership, then excellence is no longer optional.

People use GiveSendGo because it works. The platform is reliable, customer service is excellent, and processes are clean and clear. Faith does not replace competence and excellence—it demands it.

I do not actually believe there are Christian businesses. I believe there are Christian-owned businesses, and ownership brings responsibility.

You may not control the environment, but you always influence it. Influence begins with presence. You are allowed to pray before you eat lunch. You are allowed to bow your head without apology. You are allowed to respond honestly when someone asks what shapes your decisions. You are allowed to offer to pray for a coworker who shares something heavy, as long as it is done respectfully and voluntarily. You are allowed to live with kindness, steadiness, and courage that are clearly rooted in something deeper than personality. In many workplaces, faith is not explicitly forbidden, it is simply unfamiliar. Sometimes it is misunderstood. Often, fear exaggerates the limits more than policy does.

That does not mean you are reckless. Wisdom still matters. Discernment matters. You do not force conversations. You do not disregard context. You do not violate policies in the name of boldness.

But hiding faith is not the same as being wise with it.

When you do not own the platform, alignment often looks like quiet consistency: excellence in your role so that your work cannot be dismissed. Telling the truth when it would be easier to bend. Refusing to participate in cutting corners even if no one would notice. Honoring people who disagree with you. And sometimes, if we are honest, it looks like cost. You may not get the promotion. You may not be the easiest personality in the room. You may be labeled “overly principled.” In some environments, you may lose a role altogether—that possibility is real. But compartmentalization also carries a cost: silencing your relationship with God weakens clarity. Over time, you can begin to lose trust in your own voice because you have practiced muting it.

You may not own the structure, but you still own your obedience.

People spend the majority of their waking hours at work. They experience pressure, fear, conflict, loss, ambition, and burnout there. If faith only exists on Sundays, it becomes irrelevant to the places where people actually live their lives.

A revival in the marketplace does not look like sermons in boardrooms. It looks like honesty under pressure, excellence without exploitation. It looks like leaders who tell the truth when it costs them something, and businesses that operate with clarity and compassion because their owners believe they are accountable to something higher than profit.

This kind of revival does not require a stage—it requires courage.

And courage begins when we stop compartmentalizing the very thing that gives our leadership meaning.

Daniel did not wake up one day and decide to lead in a hostile culture. He was taken there.

Everything familiar was stripped away: his language, his education, his religious rhythms. Even his name was changed, an intentional attempt to redefine his identity and allegiance.

Daniel did not arrive in Babylon as a missionary. He arrived as a conquered subject. He did not get to choose his workplace, his leadership environment, or the values of the system he would serve under. He was enrolled into a government training program designed to assimilate him, retrain him, and eventually use him for Babylon’s purposes.

He had every reason to compartmentalize—to adapt fully, to blend in. To treat faith as something belonging to the past, tied to a temple that no longer stood and a city he might never see again.

And yet, Scripture tells us that early on, Daniel “resolved that he would not defile himself.”

That word “resolved” is doing heavy lifting. Before Daniel ever faced lions, he faced choices. Quiet ones. Daily ones. Decisions about what he would consume, what he would participate in, and what lines he would not cross, even when no one else seemed to care.

Daniel did not start with public courage. He started with private conviction.

He learned to live undivided in small, unseen ways long before his faith was tested publicly. Babylon tried to reprogram him. God used Babylon to refine him. Daniel’s captivity did not erase his calling, it revealed it.

Most of us did not choose the cultural moment we are leading in. We did not design the values of the systems we work within. We simply found ourselves here. Like Daniel, we are navigating workplaces, industries, and institutions that do not share our beliefs. Like Daniel, we are faced daily with the question of whether faith will be something we compartmentalize to survive, or something we carry with us to lead well.

Daniel’s story reminds us that captivity does not disqualify calling. Oftentimes it is what clarifies it.

That visibility made him a threat. Other officials, unable to find fault in his work or character, devised a plan that targeted the one place they knew Daniel would not bend: his faith. They convinced the king to issue a decree that for thirty days, no one could pray to any god or human except the king himself.

Daniel could have prayed silently. He could have adjusted for a month. He could have reasoned that God would understand. After all, this was not a denial of faith. It was just a pause. A private adjustment. A small compromise in the name of survival. But Scripture says something striking: when Daniel learned that the decree had been published, he went home and prayed just as he had done before. Not louder. Not defiantly. Not performatively. Just consistently.

Daniel did not change his behavior to make a statement. He refused to change it to preserve comfort.

We are rarely asked to renounce our faith outright. Instead, we are asked to relocate it. To make it quieter, less visible, less consequential. To keep it personal, private, and safely disconnected from our leadership.

Most of us are not facing lions. We are facing policies. Public opinion. Workplace norms. Social media scrutiny. Career risk. The cost today often looks like being passed over, being labeled difficult, being mocked or dismissed, losing a job or losing influence. Because the cost is smaller, the pressure to comply feels more reasonable.

Daniel reminds us that obedience does not always look dramatic. Sometimes it looks like refusing to edit your life when it would be easier to do so.

His faithfulness did not remove him from Babylon. It reshaped the story within it.

✦ ✦ ✦

The Truth That Replaces the Lie

The lie of compartmentalization tells us that faith must be hidden to succeed, that conviction must be muted to be credible. It tells us that the most strategic version of ourselves is the most fragmented one. But faith does not make you less credible—fragmentation does. Faith does not weaken leadership, fear does. Success that requires self-erasure is not success. It is survival dressed up as achievement.

God does not ask us to divide ourselves to be effective. He never designed faith to function as a private accessory while leadership operates independently. He invites us to trust Him with outcomes we cannot control, and to lead from the fullness of who He made us to be.

That does not mean every room will welcome it. It does not mean every audience will understand. It does not mean alignment will always feel comfortable.

It means you are no longer performing. When who you are privately and who you are publicly finally match, something shifts. Decisions become clearer. Boundaries become firmer. And compassion becomes more genuine. There is less striving to be accepted and more focus on being faithful.

And that kind of leadership—quiet, consistent, undivided— is the kind that endures.

You do not need permission to be whole. You just need the courage to stop pretending you are not.

You lead best when you are whole.

And that is the truth that replaces the lie.

Lie: I have to fight for my place.

Most division does not begin with hatred. It begins with subtle suspicion. With the moment we start bracing instead of building.

There is a narrative in the air right now. You can feel it in leadership conversations, workplace panels, conference stages, and social media feeds. It whispers that men and women are locked in a quiet rivalry, that leadership is a limited resource, and that if one rises, the other must shrink.

The moment I begin to see a man as my competition, something in my leadership shifts. I stop collaborating freely and I start guarding.

Guarded leadership feels strong on the outside, but it is defensive underneath. When we believe we are in competition, we over-prepare to avoid being questioned and over-explain to secure credibility. We start interpreting feedback as threat instead of refinement, assuming motives without ever asking questions, and bracing for dismissal before it has actually happened. Bracing changes the atmosphere of a room.

✦ ✦ ✦

Sometimes guarded leadership comes from real experiences... because these things really do happen.

There are still rooms where the men naturally talk to the men first. There are moments where you can feel it, especially in tech spaces and business environments that have historically been very male dominated. And when your business partner is also your brother, it can get complicated sometimes.

And if I am being honest, there was also this deeper feeling underneath it of wondering if he even knew that was what he was doing, or if it was just subconscious on his end.

That moment actually validated something inside of me, because when something happens repeatedly over the years, you can start questioning yourself a little bit. You start wondering if maybe you are overreacting or imagining it or being too sensitive.

Instead, I accepted his apology.

“You should lead this project.”

I looked at him, surprised.

“Why would I do that?” I asked.

He leaned back in the chair.

“Because this will be your signature initiative. This is what you’ll be known for.”

He certainly wasn’t obligated to help make it happen.

By the end of our time working together, the conflict that had once dominated our relationship was rarely mentioned. What remained was mutual respect, a successful partnership, and the memory of a project neither of us could have accomplished alone.

The issue that once threatened to divide us ultimately became the foundation for one of the most productive working relationships during my time in that position.

The Lie of Small Impact

Lie: What I’m doing doesn’t really matter.

I think leadership can quietly train you to build your life around things that feel urgent instead of things that are eternal. There is always another goal to reach, another problem to solve, another opportunity to chase, another metric telling you whether you are succeeding or falling behind. And after a while, if you are not careful, your soul starts getting discipled more by dashboards and momentum than by God.

That realization has honestly been uncomfortable for me at times because I love building things. I love vision. I love creating. I love seeing ideas come to life. Not five years from now.

Not after my career.

Actually eternal.

The crowns I get to throw at Jesus’ feet.

Scripture talks about our works being tested by fire, and there are times when that verse genuinely unsettles me. I do not think God is against success or leadership or business growth, but I do think He cares deeply about what those things are producing inside of us and through us. Success can build platforms, but it can also quietly feed pride, distraction, ego, and the false belief that visibility equals significance.

The things that seem to matter more to me now are different than what they used to be. I think about my children differently. I think about the people watching how I handle pressure differently. There is this constant pressure to keep building, keep growing, keep achieving, keep expanding. Even when you hit goals you once prayed for, the world immediately starts asking what comes next. There is never really a moment where culture tells you it is enough— enough growth, enough influence, enough success, enough visibility.

So without realizing it, it becomes easy to start attaching your value to movement. If things are growing, you feel important. If momentum slows down, you suddenly start questioning yourself. Leadership can become addictive that way because being needed feels meaningful. Being busy feels meaningful. Being visible feels meaningful.

I have seen leaders gain influence while becoming emotionally exhausted, disconnected from their families, and spiritually empty. There were moments that consumed my thoughts, kept me awake at night, and felt absolutely enormous at the time that I can barely even remember clearly anymore. Meanwhile, there are quiet moments I still think about years later because something eternal was attached to them somehow.

A prayer with someone hurting.

A difficult act of obedience.

A conversation with one of my children.

A moment where somebody encountered the love of Jesus.

A time when conviction cost something but obedience mattered more.

Those moments stay with me differently.
